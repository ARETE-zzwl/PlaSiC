# 5.2 Solar Orbital Geometry { #solar-orbital-geometry }

At every radiation step PlaSiC must answer one geometric question: where is the Sun, as seen from
each grid box? Three quantities carry the answer:

- the **solar declination** $\delta$ — the latitude where the Sun stands overhead at local noon;
- the **distance factor** $d_r=(a/r)^2$ — how strongly the eccentric orbit modulates the solar constant;
- the **cosine of the solar zenith angle** $\mu_0$ — the projection of the local vertical onto the Sun direction.

How radiation consumes these numbers is the subject of
[3.4 Radiation Parameterization](../3-parameterizations/3.4-radiation-parameterization.md)
(Eqs. 3.4.5–3.4.8); the date and clock that feed the orbit formulas come from
[5.1 Calendar](5.1-calendar.md).

## 1. The Orbit Is Frozen at Year AD 1

PlaSiC does not evaluate the Berger (1978) astronomical series during a run. PlaSiC keeps only the values the
series produces for **year AD 1** — one year after the calendar epoch (year 0, January 1) of
[5.1 Calendar](5.1-calendar.md) — and hard-codes them in `initialize_radiation()`:

**Table 5.2.1.** Orbital constants frozen at year AD 1 (single precision, set once per run).

| Quantity | Symbol | Code name | Value | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Orbital eccentricity | $e$ | `radiation.orbital_eccentricity` | 0.017465 | 1 |
| Obliquity of the ecliptic | $\epsilon$ | `radiation.obliquity_radians` | 0.413560 (23.695250°) | rad |
| Perihelion longitude + 180° | $\varpi'$ | `radiation.perihelion_plus_pi` | 4.343018 (68.836624° + 180°) | rad |
| Mean longitude at the vernal equinox | $L_0$ | `radiation.mean_longitude_equinox` | −0.0327274 | rad |
| Total solar irradiance at 1 AU | $S_0$ | `radiation.solar_constant` | 1365.0 | W m⁻² |

$L_0$ is not an independent observation; it is computed in `initialize_radiation()` from $e$,
$\varpi'$ and $\beta=\sqrt{1-e^2}$:

$$
L_0=
2\left[
\left(\frac{e}{2}+\frac{e^3}{8}\right)(1+\beta)\sin\varpi'
-\frac{e^2}{4}\left(\frac12+\beta\right)\sin 2\varpi'
+\frac{e^3}{8}\left(\frac13+\beta\right)\sin 3\varpi'
\right].
\tag{5.2.1}
$$

The truncation error of Eq. (5.2.1) is of order $e^4$, about $10^{-7}$ rad.

Three consequences deserve emphasis:

- **No Milankovitch variability.** Eccentricity, obliquity and perihelion stay at their AD 1 values
  for the whole experiment, however many model years it covers. A 1850–2014 historical run uses the
  same orbit as a single-year run.
- **The AD 1 orbit differs slightly from the modern one.** Compared with today's values, the model
  has a larger obliquity ($23.695^\circ$ vs $23.44^\circ$), a slightly larger eccentricity
  (0.017465 vs 0.0167), and a perihelion near **30 November** instead of early January; the model's
  maximum of $d_r$ falls on day ≈ 334.7.
- **The orbit follows the calendar.** The orbit period used in Eq. (5.2.3) is the number of days in
  the model year returned by `days_in_year()`, so the 360-day calendar produces a 360-day seasonal
  cycle, and the Gregorian calendar a 365- or 366-day cycle.

## 2. From the Calendar Date to Declination and Distance

At the start of every time step `plasic_runtime.c` converts the step count into a date
(`step2cal()`), and `radiation_step()` turns that date into the two orbit quantities:

```c
day = ndayofyear(plasic_calendar, plasic_nstep);
days_per_year = days_in_year(plasic_calendar, plasic_ndatim[0]);
minute_of_day = plasic_ndatim[3] * 60 + plasic_ndatim[4];
calendar_day = (float)day + (float)minute_of_day / 1440.0f;
orbital_declination(calendar_day, (float)days_per_year,
                    &declination, &distance_factor);
```

`orbital_declination()` then evaluates four short expressions. With calendar day $D$ and year
length $N_y$:

$$
D = \text{day of year} + \frac{\text{minute of day}}{1440},
\tag{5.2.2}
$$

$$
\lambda_m = L_0 + (D-80.5)\,\frac{2\pi}{N_y},
\tag{5.2.3}
$$

$$
M = \lambda_m - \varpi',
\tag{5.2.4}
$$

$$
\lambda = \lambda_m + e\left[2\sin M
+e\left(\frac54\sin 2M
+e\left(\frac{13}{12}\sin 3M-\frac14\sin M\right)\right)\right],
\tag{5.2.5}
$$

$$
\delta = \arcsin\!\left(\sin\epsilon\,\sin\lambda\right),
\qquad
d_r = \left[\frac{1+e\cos(\lambda-\varpi')}{1-e^2}\right]^{\,2}.
\tag{5.2.6}
$$

The remaining error in $\lambda$ is $O(e^4)\approx10^{-7}$ rad, far below the
resolution of $D$, which is one minute. The distance factor in Eq. (5.2.6) is the inverse square of
the normalised Sun–Earth distance $r/a$ obtained from the conic orbit equation; $d_r>1$ at
perihelion, $d_r<1$ at aphelion.

Evaluated with the AD 1 constants of Table 5.2.1, the orbit places the cardinal points as follows
(day numbers and dates for a common, non-leap year):

**Table 5.2.2.** Cardinal points of the frozen model orbit.

| Event | True longitude | Quantity | Day | Date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| March equinox | $\lambda=0$ | $\delta=0$ | 80.5 | 21 March |
| Aphelion | $\lambda=\varpi'-180^\circ$ | $d_r=0.96596$ | 152.2 | 1 June |
| June solstice | $\lambda=90^\circ$ | $\delta=+23.695^\circ$ | 174.4 | 23 June |
| September equinox | $\lambda=180^\circ$ | $\delta=0$ | 266.8 | 23 September |
| Perihelion | $\lambda=\varpi'$ | $d_r=1.03587$ | 334.7 | 30 November |
| December solstice | $\lambda=270^\circ$ | $\delta=-23.695^\circ$ | 355.4 | 21 December |

The extremes of $d_r$ are $+3.59\%$ and $-3.40\%$ relative to the 1 AU value, so the top-of-atmosphere
flux of Eq. (3.4.8) varies by about $\pm3.5\%$ over the year on top of the declination cycle.

## 3. From Declination to the Local Zenith Angle

`rad_solar_angles()` combines the date-level $\delta$ and $d_r$ with the clock and the grid. For
longitude index $j=0,\dots,N_{\mathrm{lon}}-1$ (the grid starts at 0°E and runs east) and minute of day $m$:

$$
h = m\,\frac{2\pi}{1440} + j\,\frac{2\pi}{N_{\mathrm{lon}}} - \pi,
\tag{5.2.7}
$$

$$
\mu_0 = \max\!\left(0,\;\sin\varphi\,\sin\delta
+ \cos\varphi\,\cos\delta\,\cos h\right).
\tag{5.2.8}
$$

Equation (5.2.7) is the local **hour angle**: $h=0$ at local noon and increases through the
afternoon. The model clock is UTC and the grid longitude supplies the local-time offset, so at
12:00 UTC the subsolar meridian is Greenwich. The
`max(0, ·)` implements night, so a grid box with the Sun below the horizon receives exactly zero
shortwave radiation, and polar night stays dark for the whole day. 

Every grid box shares the same $\delta$ and $d_r$ — the Sun is
treated as a point source at infinity — and differs only in $h$ and $\varphi$. The routine stores
$\mu_0$ in `radiation.cosine_zenith` and $d_r$ in `radiation.sun_distance_factor`, and
`rad_shortwave()` evaluates the two-band top-of-atmosphere flux of Eq. (3.4.8):

$$
F_{0,b} = w_b\,S_0\,d_r\,\mu_0,
\qquad w_1=0.517,\; w_2=0.483.
\tag{5.2.9}
$$

Because the hour angle is recomputed from `minute_of_day` at every step, the diurnal cycle is sampled
`plasic_ntspd` times per day.

## 4. What the Model Orbit Does Not Include

- **Orbital evolution.** $e$, $\epsilon$ and $\varpi'$ are constants of the run; there is no
  Milankovitch forcing, and no way to change the orbit from the CLI or the JSON configuration.
- **Equation of time.** The hour angle advances uniformly in mean solar time, so local noon is
  always 12:00; the true Sun's departure from mean solar time (up to about ±16 minutes) is absent.
- **Annual drift with the calendar.** The equinox is fixed at day 80.5 every year, so in leap years
  the corresponding calendar date shifts by one day; PlaSiC accepts this one-day seasonal drift
  rather than re-anchoring the orbit each year.

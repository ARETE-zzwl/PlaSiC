# 4.1 How the Spheres Are Coupled { #_1 }

This note explains: how the atmosphere sends forcing to
the land surface, sea ice and ocean; how the surface returns boundary
conditions; at what frequency each group of variables is exchanged. 

## 1. There is no separate "master coupler" in the code

PlaSiC does not couple its components through files or through a single
`coupler` object. The modules are called in a fixed order inside
`plasic_physics_step()` (`physics_step()` in `src/physics/physics_processes.c`)
and exchange variables through shared grid-point arrays:

```text
post-adiabatic dimensional grid-point state
  -> moisture_fixer()             remove negative humidity (optional)
  -> surface_fluxes()             surface fluxes over land and sea
  -> vertical_diffusion_step()    boundary-layer vertical diffusion
  -> radiation_step()             radiative fluxes and heating rates
  -> rain_step()                  rain, snow and clouds
  -> land_step()                  update land points only
  -> sea_step()                   update ocean points only; call ice_step()/ocean_step() when due
  (grid-point tendencies are then transformed to spectral form)
  -> runtime_spectral_diabatic()  add this step's physics tendencies to the spectral state
```

The call order is the coupling order (cf. the sequential atmosphere–ocean coupling of
Manabe & Bryan, 1969, and the PlaSim model design of Fraedrich et al., 2005). Horizontal points are split by the binary
land–sea mask `dls`:

- `dls = 1`: land points, updated by `land_step()`;
- `dls < 0.5`: ocean points, updated by `sea_step()`, `ice_step()` and
  `ocean_step()`.

At initialization the fractional `land_sea_mask` field is rounded to 0 or 1 in
`load_surface_initialization()` (`physics_initialization.c`), so every point
belongs to exactly one of the two classes and the land and sea branches never
compete for the same surface state.

Figure 4.1.1. Coupling variables and directions between the atmosphere, land, sea ice and ocean.

The "sea-surface interface" in Figure 4.1.1 is essential: the atmosphere never
calls the mixed-layer ocean directly. Atmospheric fluxes are first accumulated
by `sea_step()` and handed to the sea ice; the ice reorganizes the heat,
freshwater and stress forcing and passes it to the ocean; the ocean returns SST,
mixed-layer depth and its heat flux to the ice, and those values must still pass
back through the ice and the sea-surface interface before they become surface
boundary conditions the atmosphere can use.

## 2. What each side of the coupling boundary stores

### 2.1 Atmosphere side: lowest model level and surface diagnostic layer

`gridpointd_physical()` restores the spectral variables of the dynamical core to
dimensional grid-point fields. The surface-flux calculation (Louis, 1979) mainly reads:

| Variable | Meaning | Role in the coupling |
|---|---|---|
| `dt[low]`, `dq[low]` | temperature and specific humidity of the lowest atmospheric model level | their difference from the surface state drives sensible and latent heat exchange |
| `du[low]`, `dv[low]` | horizontal wind at the lowest model level | sets the turbulent exchange strength and the surface stress |
| `dp` | surface pressure | used for air density, saturation specific humidity and flux conversion |
| `dt[surface]`, `dq[surface]` | surface temperature and surface saturation specific humidity | boundary state for land or sea points |
| `dwetfac` | surface moisture availability factor | limits how much moisture evaporation can take from the surface |
| `dalb` | surface albedo | enters the shortwave radiation calculation |
| `dz0` | surface roughness length | enters the exchange coefficients for heat, moisture and momentum |

!!! warning "`dtsa` is not the surface temperature"

    `dtsa` is the **near-surface air temperature** that
    `flux_surface_exchange_coefficients()` obtains by extrapolating the lowest
    atmospheric model level downward (with the virtual-temperature correction
    used by the default `flux.surface_temperature_mode = 2`). The actual land
    or sea surface temperature is `dt[surface]`, written by
    `land_surface_exchange()` or `sea_update_surface()`. The difference between
    the two is the main driver of the sensible heat exchange.

### 2.2 Surface side: slow state with memory

The surface stores state that persists across time steps and must be written to
the restart file:

| Component | Main state | Converted into, before returning to the atmosphere |
|---|---|---|
| Land | surface temperature, multi-layer soil temperature, soil water, snow | `dt[surface]`, `dq[surface]`, `dwetfac`, `dalb`, `dz0` |
| Sea ice | ice surface temperature, ice thickness, ice cover, snow on ice | surface temperature at ocean points, plus contributions to the humidity, albedo and roughness blends |
| Ocean | ocean temperature, mixed-layer depth, ocean heat storage | SST, mixed-layer depth and the ocean heat flux passed to the sea ice |

These slow states never overwrite the atmospheric spectral temperature or
humidity directly. They first become surface boundary conditions; the next call
to `surface_fluxes()`, `vertical_diffusion_step()` and `radiation_step()`
converts the boundary differences into temperature, moisture and momentum
tendencies of the atmosphere.

## 3. What the atmosphere sends to the surface

One atmospheric physics step first computes surface fluxes, radiation and
precipitation; the land and sea branches then consume these results.

### 3.1 Atmosphere to land

`land_step()` receives the following forcing for the current step:

| Code variable | Physical quantity | How the land uses it |
|---|---|---|
| `dshfl`, `dlhfl` | sensible and latent heat fluxes | enter the surface and soil energy budgets |
| `dflux[surface]` | net surface radiative flux, i.e. shortwave plus longwave | heats or cools the land surface layer |
| `dprl`, `dprc` | large-scale and convective precipitation | feed snow and soil water |
| `dprs` | snowfall subset of the precipitation above | updates snow separately, so snow is not treated as liquid soil water |
| `devap` | evaporation water flux | removes water from the land water budget |
| `dp` | surface pressure | used to compute the saturation specific humidity at the land temperature |

Land temperature has two paths:

- if `land.prognostic_temperature != 0`, `land_tands()` (**Temperature And
  Snow**) uses the energy and water forcing above to advance the surface
  temperature, soil temperature, snow temperature and snow depth;
- otherwise, `land_prescribed_temperature()` simply reads the
  monthly-interpolated climatological temperature and does not integrate a land
  heat budget.

Land water also has two paths:

- if `land.prognostic_water != 0`, the soil-water bucket is advanced by
  `land_update_soil_water()`, which integrates the surface water flux
  (evaporation plus rain, minus the part of precipitation stored as snow) and
  clamps the bucket to \([0, \text{field capacity}]\); water beyond field
  capacity leaves the modeled system;
- otherwise, `land_prescribed_water()` fills the soil water with the
  monthly-interpolated prescribed wetness.

The defaults are `prognostic_temperature = 1` and `prognostic_water = 1`, so the
two prognostic paths are normally used.

### 3.2 Atmosphere to sea surface, sea ice and ocean

`sea_prepare_step()` samples and accumulates the following quantities at ocean
points on every atmospheric step:

| Accumulated quantity | Composition | Final destination |
|---|---|---|
| total surface heat flux | `dshfl + dswfl[surface] + dlwfl[surface] + dlhfl` | surface energy forcing of the sea ice |
| freshwater flux | `dprl + dprc + devap` | sea-ice/ocean freshwater budget |
| solid precipitation | `dprs` | snow on sea ice |
| surface stress | `dtaux`, `dtauy` | momentum forcing of the sea ice and ocean |
| friction forcing | `dust3` | friction velocity cubed \(u_*^3\), passed to the ocean |
| heat-flux components | `dshfl`, `dlhfl`, `dswfl[surface]`, `dlwfl[surface]` and the derivatives `dshdt`, `dlhdt` | accumulated and stored under the same averaging window (diagnostics and restart bookkeeping) |

When the coupling instant arrives, the accumulated sums are divided by the
number of samples to form arithmetic means over the whole coupling window.
`ice_step()` then reads these mean forcings. In other words, when the coupling
interval is longer than one atmospheric step, the slow components do not see one
instantaneous atmospheric step but the average heat, freshwater and momentum
forcing over the window.

## 4. How the surface returns its influence to the atmosphere

### 4.1 Boundary conditions returned by the land

After the land state is updated, `land_surface_exchange()` translates the
internal land state into the atmospheric interface variables:

1. it writes `land.surface_temperature` into `dt[surface]`;
2. it computes the saturation specific humidity from the surface temperature and
   surface pressure (converting the saturation mixing ratio to specific
   humidity) and writes it into `dq[surface]`;
3. it builds `dwetfac` from the ratio of soil water to field capacity; when snow
   is present, surface moisture supply is not limited by soil dryness and
   `dwetfac` is set to 1;
4. it combines the background albedo, snow amount and snow temperature into
   `dalb`;
5. it updates the snow and soil-temperature diagnostics. `dz0` is built once at
   initialization from the prescribed roughness field and stays fixed during
   the subsequent land time steps.

A land drought therefore does not subtract a fixed amount from the atmospheric
`dq`; it first lowers `dwetfac`, so the evaporation computed by
`flux_mkevap()` and the resulting atmospheric moisture tendency become smaller
in the next step. In other words, `dq[surface]` always holds the saturation
value; the moisture-availability limitation is imposed by `dwetfac` on the
exchange coefficient, not by modifying this boundary humidity.

Glacier points get a further override: `land_apply_glacier_surface()` replaces
the surface albedo with the temperature-dependent glacier albedo (clamped
between the glacier minimum and maximum) and sets `dwetfac` to 1.

### 4.2 State returned by the sea ice and ocean

After the sea ice is updated, `ice_step()` writes the following state into the
`sea.coupled_*` fields that are handed to `sea_finish_coupling()`:

- the sea surface temperature `SST` and the actual surface temperature `Ts`;
- the mixed-layer depth `MLD`;
- the sea-ice cover, sea-ice thickness and snow on ice;
- diagnostics such as the snow melt rate and the snow depth change rate.

`sea_finish_coupling()` copies them into the atmosphere-side ocean-point arrays
and clears the flux accumulators of the previous coupling window. Afterwards,
whether or not the sea ice/ocean update was triggered on this step,
`sea_update_surface()` refreshes the atmospheric boundary from the currently
available sea-surface state. Note that the atmosphere-side surface temperature
is \(T_s\) (the ice surface temperature where ice is present, and the SST at
open-water points), not the open-ocean SST itself.

For an ice cover \(c_i\), several key boundary quantities at an ocean point are
blended between open water and sea ice:

$$
r_h=(1-c_i)r_{h,\mathrm{sea}}+c_i r_{h,\mathrm{ice}},
$$

$$
\alpha=(1-c_i)\alpha_{\mathrm{sea}}
+c_i\min\left[\alpha_{\mathrm{ice}},\ 0.5+0.025(273-T_s)\right],
$$

$$
z_0=(1-c_i)z_{0,\mathrm{sea}}^{*}+c_i z_{0,\mathrm{ice}}.
$$

Here \(r_h\) is the humidity factor `dwetfac` (both the open-water and the ice
value default to 1, so this blend matters only for non-default configurations),
and the dynamic open-water roughness \(z_{0,\mathrm{sea}}^{*}\) is computed from
the surface stress through the Charnock relation (Charnock, 1955) with a minimum value:

$$
z_{0,\mathrm{sea}}^{*}
=\max\left[
\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{Ch}}\,\lvert\boldsymbol{\tau}\rvert\,R_d T_s}{g\,p_s},
\; z_{0,\mathrm{sea}}
\right].
$$

An increase in sea-ice cover therefore changes, all at the same time:

- the surface albedo, and thus the shortwave radiation absorbed in the next
  step;
- the surface roughness, and thus the momentum and turbulent heat exchange of
  the next step;
- the surface temperature and humidity boundary, and thus the sensible and
  latent heat fluxes of the next step.

This is how "sea ice influences the atmosphere" is implemented in the code: the
sea-ice module does not modify the atmospheric spectral fields directly. It
changes the surface boundary, and the next atmospheric physics step converts the
boundary change into tendencies.

### 4.3 Land water closes inside the land column

In the current code, precipitation that falls on land does not leave the land
component. `land_update_soil_water()` integrates the soil-water bucket and then
clamps it to \([0, \text{field capacity}]\); water in excess of field capacity
simply leaves the modeled system. There is no runoff field passed to `sea_prepare_step()`: the ocean freshwater budget
is built from precipitation, evaporation and snowfall only.

## 6. Why sea ice sits between the atmosphere and the ocean

The ocean does not consume the atmospheric fluxes of `sea_prepare_step()`
directly. When the atmosphere–ice coupling instant arrives:

```text
coupling-window mean fluxes from the atmosphere
  -> ice_step()
       ├─ update the sea-ice state
       ├─ accumulate the heat, freshwater, stress and friction forcing sent to the ocean
       └─ at the ice–ocean coupling instant, call ocean_step()
              -> returns SST, mixed-layer depth and ocean heat flux
  -> the ice completes its surface state with the ocean return values
  -> sea_finish_coupling()
  -> sea_update_surface()
  -> atmospheric boundary for the next step
```

Sea ice thus has two interface roles:

- upward, it converts the ocean and ice-surface state into surface temperature,
  albedo, humidity factor and roughness that the atmosphere can use;
- downward, it reorganizes the atmospheric heat, freshwater and momentum
  forcing before handing it to the ocean, and receives the ocean's SST and
  heat-flux feedback.

## 7. Coupling frequency

The atmospheric time step is

$$
\Delta t=\frac{\text{solar day}}{\text{ntspd}}.
$$

For the default Earth/T42 configuration (`ntspd = 48`),
\(\Delta t = 1800\,\mathrm{s} = 30\,\mathrm{min}\).

| Process | Trigger | Current default | T42 equivalent frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atmospheric physics and surface fluxes | every `plasic_physics_step()` | every step | 30 min |
| Boundary-layer vertical diffusion `vertical_diffusion_step()` | every `physics_step()`, switch `flux.vertical_diffusion` | every step | 30 min |
| Land `land_step()` | no extra interval test | every step | 30 min |
| Sea-surface flux sampling | every `sea_prepare_step()` | every step | 30 min |
| Atmosphere–sea ice | `nstep % sea.coupling_interval == 0` and `nkits == 0` | `coupling_interval = 1` | 30 min |
| Sea ice–ocean | only inside `ice_step()`, and `nstep % ice.ocean_coupling_interval == 0` | `ocean_coupling_interval = 1` | 30 min |

The extra `nkits == 0` condition postpones coupling until the explicit Euler
start-up of the leapfrog scheme has finished (Robert, 1966; Asselin, 1972).

!!! note "`output_interval = 32` is not a coupling frequency"

    `ice.output_interval` and `ocean.output_interval` control when diagnostic
    accumulators are reset or written; they do not decide when the sea ice and
    the ocean exchange state. The actual coupling intervals are the two
    `coupling_interval` values in the table above.

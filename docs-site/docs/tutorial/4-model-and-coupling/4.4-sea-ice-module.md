# 4.4 Thermodynamic Sea Ice

This chapter describes the thermodynamic sea-ice kernel of PlaSiC: it updates the ice/snow surface temperature, the snow amount, and the ice thickness once per coupling step. The atmosphere-facing state it operates on is prepared by the sea interface in [4.3 Sea Interface](4.3-sea-interface.md), and the mixed-layer ocean it exchanges heat with is updated by the slab ocean in [4.5 Slab Ocean](4.5-slab-ocean.md).

The ice kernels implement a zero-layer thermodynamic model with one effective surface heat capacity. They do not predict ice motion, horizontal ice transport, ridging, or internal stress. The update order in `physics_processes.c` is shown in the below flow chart.

## 1. State and sign conventions

![Zero-layer thermodynamic sea-ice column: atmosphere, snow, ice and ocean with the state variables and the net surface heat flux](<../assets/images/sea_ice_illustration.png>)

Figure 4.4.2. The zero-layer thermodynamic sea-ice column. 

The ice module owns this part of the coupled surface state:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $T_i$ | Temperature of the ice/snow surface seen by the atmosphere |
| $T_o$ | SST of the mixed-layer ocean, the lower boundary of the ice column |
| $h_i$ | Sea-ice thickness |
| $c_i^*$ | Continuous internal ice compactness used to expand or retreat the ice edge |
| $c_i$ | Binary coupling mask applied by the current configuration |
| $S$ | Snow water equivalent on sea ice |

The full coupled-state table, including how these fields are handed back to the atmosphere, is in [4.3 Sea Interface](4.3-sea-interface.md).

For the ice ledger, $Q_{ice}>0$ melts ice and $Q_{ice}<0$ forms ice. The latent-heat relation is therefore

$$
 h_i^{n+1}=h_i^n-
 \frac{Q_{ice}\,\Delta t}{\rho_iL_i}.
$$

The default values used by the kernels include $\rho_w=1000\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $\rho_o=1030\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $\rho_i=920\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $\rho_s=330\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $L_i=3.28\times10^5\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$, and $L_s=3.337\times10^5\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$.

## 2. Effective snow depth and conduction

The prognostic snow variable is a water equivalent, not a geometric depth. The geometric depth is

$$
 h_s=\frac{\rho_w}{\rho_s}S.
$$

For an ice column thick enough to resolve, the kernels form an effective conductivity and conductance,

$$
 \bar{k}=\frac{k_i h_i+k_s h_s}{h_i+h_s},
 \qquad
 K_z=\frac{\bar{k}}{h_i+h_s}.
$$

More snow usually reduces $K_z$ because snow is less conductive than ice. For the default maximum-thickness parameter ($h_i\ge 9\ \mathrm{m}$), the effective vertical conductance is set to zero.

## 3. Surface temperature

For $h_i\ge h_{min}=0.1\ \mathrm{m}$, the surface uses the effective heat capacity

$$
 C_i=\rho_i c_{p,i}h_{min}.
$$

The semi-implicit surface-energy equation is

$$
 C_i\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}
 =Q_a-K_z(T_i^{n+1}-T_o^n),
$$

so

$$
 T_i^{n+1}=\frac{(C_i/\Delta t)T_i^n+Q_a+K_zT_o^n}
 {(C_i/\Delta t)+K_z}.
$$

Using the new-time temperature in the conduction term makes this update less sensitive to short-step oscillations. If the trial value exceeds the snow/ice melting point $T_m=273.16\ \mathrm{K}$, the kernel sets $T_i=T_m$ and reports the surplus

$$
 Q_m=Q_a+K_z(T_o-T_m)-\frac{C_i}{\Delta t}(T_m-T_i^n).
$$

Positive $Q_m$ is used first to melt snow. Only the remainder can melt sea ice. For thin or absent ice ($h_i<h_{min}$), the model sets $T_i=T_o$ and treats a positive atmospheric flux as available melt energy.

## 4. Snow melt and snow-to-ice conversion

When the ice mask is below its snow-retention threshold, snowfall is treated as falling on open water. Its latent heat is removed immediately from the surface budget. On ice, the order is always **melt snow first, then melt ice**:

$$
 M_s=\min\left(\frac{S}{\Delta t},\frac{Q_m}{\rho_wL_s}\right).
$$

Any part of $Q_m$ left after $S$ reaches zero is returned to the ice ledger as residual melt energy $Q_m^{rem}$.

Heavy snow can flood the snow/ice interface. From the buoyancy balance,

$$
 D=\frac{\rho_i h_i+\rho_w S}{\rho_o},
$$

flooding begins when $D>h_i$, or equivalently

$$
 \rho_wS>(\rho_o-\rho_i)h_i.
$$

The flooded snow is converted to ice. The target thickness that restores the snow/ice interface to sea level is

$$
 h_i^{target}=\frac{\rho_i h_i+\rho_wS}{\rho_o}.
$$

Define the target thickness increment and the corresponding change in snow water equivalent as

$$
 \Delta h_i^*=h_i^{target}-h_i
 =\frac{\rho_wS-(\rho_o-\rho_i)h_i}{\rho_o},
 \qquad
 \Delta S=-\frac{\rho_i}{\rho_w}\Delta h_i^*.
$$

The code converts this snow change into the snow-to-ice latent-heat flux

$$
 \boxed{
 Q_{s\to i}=\frac{\rho_w L_s\Delta S}{\Delta t}
 =-\frac{\rho_iL_s}{\Delta t}\Delta h_i^*
 =-\frac{\rho_iL_s}{\rho_o\Delta t}
 \left[\rho_wS-(\rho_o-\rho_i)h_i\right]
 }
$$

when the flooding condition is true; otherwise $Q_{s\to i}=0$. Here $S$ is the snow amount after snowfall has been added and surface melt has been removed. Because $\Delta S<0$ during conversion, $Q_{s\to i}<0$: with the model sign convention, it is a freezing contribution that increases the ice thickness. The target thickness is not assigned directly; `ice_snow()` updates $S$ and passes this flux to `ice_make_ice()`, where it is combined with the other terms in the ice ledger.

For example, take $h_i=1.0\ \mathrm{m}$, $S=0.20\ \mathrm{m}$, and the default 32 steps per day ($\Delta t=2700\ \mathrm{s}$). The flooding threshold is

$$
 S_{crit}=\frac{\rho_o-\rho_i}{\rho_w}h_i=0.11\ \mathrm{m},
$$

so conversion occurs. The target increment and snow change are

$$
 \Delta h_i^*=0.08738\ \mathrm{m},
 \qquad
 \Delta S=-0.08039\ \mathrm{m},
$$

which gives

$$
 Q_{s\to i}
 =\frac{1000\times3.337\times10^5\times(-0.08039)}{2700}
 \simeq -9.94\times10^3\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}.
$$

The negative sign means that this term acts to grow ice in the subsequent `ice_make_ice()` update.

Since the implementation uses $L_s$ to form $Q_{s\to i}$ but uses $L_i$ when converting the total ice flux back to thickness, the isolated contribution to the actual prognostic thickness is $(L_s/L_i)\Delta h_i^*$ (about $0.08890\ \mathrm{m}$ in this example), rather than exactly $\Delta h_i^*$. Thus the formula is the code's energy-ledger definition; with the default unequal $L_s$ and $L_i$, the resulting snow-to-ice mass mapping is not exactly conservative, with a discrepancy of about $1.74\%$.

## 5. Ice-thickness energy ledger

For the default prognostic, resolved-ice path, the complete phase-change flux used by `ice_make_ice()` is

$$
 Q_{ice}=K_z(T_i-T_f)+Q_{o\to i}+Q_m^{rem}+Q_{s\to i},
$$

where $T_f=271.25\ \mathrm{K}$ is the saline-water freezing point. Thus $T_i<T_f$ favours growth and $T_i>T_f$ favours melt. $Q_{o\to i}$ is the correction returned by the slab ocean when its trial SST would cross $T_f$; it converts ocean sensible heat into ice latent heat without discarding energy.

If a trial update would make $h_i<0$, `ice_make_ice()` clips the thickness at zero and returns the surplus melting energy to the ocean budget.

The upper-thickness limit is handled after this update. If the trial thickness exceeds the configured $h_{max}$, the excess thickness and its latent-heat-equivalent flux are

$$
 \Delta h_{excess}=h_i^{trial}-h_{max},
 \qquad
 Q_{max}=\frac{\rho_iL_i}{\Delta t}\Delta h_{excess}.
$$

The model then sets $h_i=h_{max}$, stores $Q_{max}$ in `maximum_thickness_flux`, and adds it to the ice-melt diagnostic. Thus the thickness is still clipped, but the energy equivalent of the removed thickness is explicitly recorded instead of disappearing from the diagnostics.

For example, with the default $h_{max}=9.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and $\Delta t=2700\ \mathrm{s}$, a trial thickness of $9.1\ \mathrm{m}$ gives

$$
 Q_{max}
 =\frac{920\times3.28\times10^5\times(9.1-9.0)}{2700}
 \simeq1.12\times10^4\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}.
$$

This is positive because it is the equivalent melting flux required to cancel the extra $0.1\ \mathrm{m}$ of ice growth. With `maximum_thickness_correction` enabled, the current C implementation carries this value into the next ice step as

$$
 Q_a^{modified}=Q_a-Q_{max}.
$$

## 6. Compactness and the ice mask

`ice_compactness()` keeps a continuous compactness $c_i^*$ to represent low-cost ice-edge growth and retreat. Here $h_r=0.5\ \mathrm{m}$ is an empirical **reference thickness increment**. When the ice grows by $\Delta h_i=h_i^{n+1}-h_i^n>0$, the update is

$$
 c_i^{*,n+1}
 =\min\left[1,\ c_i^{*,n}
 +(1-c_i^{*,n})\frac{\Delta h_i}{h_r}\right].
$$

Thus $\Delta h_i/h_r$ is the fraction of the still-uncovered part of the grid cell that becomes compact ice during the step. For example, if $c_i^*=0.4$ and the ice grows by $0.1\ \mathrm{m}$, then

$$
 c_i^{*,n+1}=0.4+(1-0.4)\frac{0.1}{0.5}=0.52.
$$

An increment $\Delta h_i=h_r$ would close all of the remaining open fraction in one update. Therefore, a smaller $h_r$ produces faster lateral ice-edge expansion for the same thickness growth, whereas a larger $h_r$ produces slower expansion. The parameter is used only in the growth branch. When the ice thins, compactness instead decreases according to the relative thickness loss,

$$
 c_i^{*,n+1}
 =\max\left[0,\ c_i^{*,n}
 \left(1+\frac{h_i^{n+1}-h_i^n}{2h_i^n}\right)\right],
$$

and is set to zero if the ice disappears. The current prognostic configuration then applies its cover threshold to obtain the binary coupling mask $c_i$.

This compactness is an edge parameterization. It is not a resolved sub-grid distribution of floes, and there is no ice drift or convergence/divergence.

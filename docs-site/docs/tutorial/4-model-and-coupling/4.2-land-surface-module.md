# 4.2 Land Surface Module { #_1 }

The land module of PlaSiC compresses relevant processes into a land surface system with very low computational cost: **one surface temperature, five soil layer temperatures, one soil water bucket and one snow reservoir**. It does not attempt to represent vegetation, roots or complex groundwater explicitly; instead it keeps the three "memories" that matter most for climate feedbacks (in the bucket-hydrology lineage of Manabe, 1969; see Pitman, 2003, for a review of land-surface schemes):

- soil temperature stores the **thermal memory** from diurnal to seasonal time scales;
- soil water stores the **liquid water memory** between precipitation and evaporation;
- snow stores the **frozen water memory** of solid precipitation and melt.

## 0. The Global Picture: How One Land Time Step Is Computed

![Detailed algorithm flow of a single physical time step of the PlaSiC land module](<../assets/images/land-surface-algorithm-flow.svg>)

Figure 4.2.1. Flow chart in one land update. Orange nodes handle energy, blue nodes handle water, and green nodes convert internal states into the atmospheric boundary. 

Compressed into one sentence, the flow chart says:

> **First update the internal thermal and water reservoirs of the land with this step's fluxes, then
> diagnose the new state into the temperature, humidity, albedo and roughness visible to the next
> atmosphere step.**

## 1. Which Physical States a Land Point Stores

For each land grid point, define

| Symbol | Physical meaning |
|---|---|
| $T_s$ | Surface or snow-surface temperature |
| $T_k, k=1,\ldots,5$ | Temperatures of the five soil layers |
| $W$ | Soil water content per unit area |
| $S$ | Snow water equivalent |
| $T_{\rm snow}$ | Diagnostic snow temperature |

Here $S$ is the **snow water equivalent**, not the geometric snow depth. With water density $\rho_w$ and snow density $\rho_s$, the corresponding physical snow depth is

$$
h_s=\frac{\rho_w}{\rho_s}S.
\tag{4.2.1}
$$

The default is $\rho_s=330\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, so the same water equivalent corresponds to a snow layer about three times as thick. 

$T_{\rm snow}$ is a diagnostic, not a prognostic variable: it is set to the melting point while the snow reservoir is empty and to the surface temperature $T_s$ otherwise.

Both the land temperature and the soil water can be switched to prescribed monthly climatologies. By default, however, both are predicted, so the following discussion focuses on the fully prognostic land surface.

The most common default parameters in the current C implementation are listed below. $C$ denotes a **volumetric heat capacity**, so its units are $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$, not the specific heat capacity $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

| Parameter | Default value | Role |
|---|---:|---|
| $h_m$ | $0.20\ \mathrm{m}$ | Depth of the thermally active surface layer |
| $W_{\max}$ | $0.50\ \mathrm{m}$ | Soil water bucket capacity |
| $S_{\max}$ | $5.0\ \mathrm{m}$ water equivalent | Upper limit of the snow reservoir |
| $C_{\rm soil}$ | $2.40\times10^6\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$ | Volumetric heat capacity of ordinary soil |
| $C_{\rm ice}$ | $2.07\times10^6\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$ | Volumetric heat capacity at glacier points |
| $C_{\rm snow}$ | $0.6897\times10^6\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$ | Volumetric heat capacity of snow |
| $K_{\rm soil}$ | $1.8\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ | Thermal conductivity of ordinary soil |
| $K_{\rm ice}$ | $2.03\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ | Thermal conductivity at glacier points |
| $K_{\rm snow}$ | $0.31\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ | Thermal conductivity of snow |

These defaults are set in `initialize_land_defaults()` (`src/physics/physics_initialization.c`); quantities that vary by grid point, such as $W_{\max}$, are read from the surface boundary data.

## 2. A Single Flux Sign Convention: Positive into the Land

Let the total energy flux delivered to the land by the atmosphere and radiation be

$$
Q_a=R_n+H+LE,
\tag{4.2.2}
$$

where

- $R_n$ is the net surface shortwave plus net longwave radiation;
- $H$ is the sensible heat flux;
- $LE$ is the latent heat flux.

In the land energy equation, **energy flowing into the surface is positive**. Hence $R_n>0$ when solar radiation heats the ground, $H<0$ when the ground is warmer than the air and transports sensible heat upward, and $LE<0$ when evaporation removes energy.

Water fluxes use the same "positive into the land" convention. Let the rain and snowfall rates be $P_r$ and $P_s$, and let the evaporation flux be $E$; then $E<0$ during evaporation. Precipitation therefore increases the land water content and evaporation is subtracted from the reservoir automatically, with no extra sign changes in the individual equations.

## 3. The Surface Is Not a Zero-Heat-Capacity Boundary: Thin-Layer Energy Balance

### 3.1 Why a Surface Layer Is Necessary

If the surface temperature were required to satisfy an instantaneous balance,

$$
0=Q_a-G,
$$

then even a small flux perturbation could make $T_s$ jump sharply between adjacent time steps. PlaSiC therefore gives the surface a thermally active layer of thickness $h_m$ and stores the previous temperature in its areal heat capacity $C_mh_m$ (the force–restore representation of Bhumralkar, 1975, and Deardorff, 1978). The continuous form is

$$
C_mh_m\frac{dT_s}{dt}=Q_a-G-M,
\tag{4.2.3}
$$

where $G$ is the heat flux conducted from the surface into the first soil layer, and $M$ (written $Q_m$ in Section 6) is the energy consumed by snowmelt. The default is $h_m=0.20\ \mathrm{m}$. Here $C_m$ is the **effective volumetric heat capacity of the surface layer**: it equals the soil value on bare ground, the ice value at glacier points, and the snow–soil blend of Section 4 when snow is present.

When there is no melting, the heat conduction between the surface and the first soil layer is approximated over the center-to-center distance:

$$
G=\frac{2K_1^*}{h_1^*}(T_s-T_1),
\tag{4.2.4}
$$

where $K_1^*$ and $h_1^*$ are the effective conductivity and effective thickness of the first combined layer after accounting for snow cover. When $T_s>T_1$, $G>0$ and energy leaves the surface into the soil.

### 3.2 How the Semi-Implicit Update Damps Numerical Oscillations

PlaSiC evaluates the surface side of (4.2.4) at the new time level using $T_s^{n+1}$ (and sets $M=0$ here, leaving melting to the separate step in Section 6), which gives

$$
T_s^{n+1}=
\frac{
\dfrac{C_mh_m}{\Delta t}T_s^n
+Q_a^n
+\dfrac{2K_1^*}{h_1^*}T_1^n
}{
\dfrac{C_mh_m}{\Delta t}
+\dfrac{2K_1^*}{h_1^*}
}.
\tag{4.2.5}
$$

Equation (4.2.5) can be read as a competition between three temperature controls:

- the larger $C_mh_m/\Delta t$ is, the more the surface tends to keep its old temperature;
- $Q_a$ determines the external heating or cooling during this step;
- the larger $2K_1^*/h_1^*$ is, the faster the surface is pulled back toward the first soil temperature.

The surface can therefore respond quickly to diurnal flux variations without detaching itself from the thermal inertia of the soil below.

## 4. How Snow Alters the Surface Thermal Properties

The thermal conductivity of snow is much smaller than that of soil: by default,
following the snow thermal properties summarized by Yen (1981),

$$
K_{\rm snow}=0.31\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}},
\qquad
K_{\rm soil}=1.8\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
$$

Snow is thus first of all a layer of insulation. The model does not, however, add the full snow depth directly to the soil grid. Instead it splits the physical snow depth into two parts:

$$
h_{\rm mix}=\min(h_s,h_m),
\qquad
h_{\rm ins}=\min(h_s,1\ {\rm m})-h_{\rm mix}.
$$

Snow up to the thickness of the surface active layer $h_m$ enters the effective heat capacity of that layer; snow above that thickness, up to $1\ {\rm m}$, becomes an explicit insulating layer between the surface and the first soil layer. The effective volumetric heat capacity of the surface active layer is

$$
C_m=
\frac{C_{\rm soil}C_{\rm snow}h_m}
{C_{\rm soil}h_{\rm mix}
+C_{\rm snow}(h_m-h_{\rm mix})}.
$$

Writing it in reciprocal form makes the mixing logic clearer:

$$
\frac{1}{C_m}
=\frac{h_{\rm mix}}{h_m}\frac{1}{C_{\rm snow}}
+\frac{h_m-h_{\rm mix}}{h_m}\frac{1}{C_{\rm soil}}.
$$

It is therefore a harmonic-type blend that weights $1/C$ by thickness fraction, not a simple arithmetic mean. The expression returns $C_{\rm soil}$ when there is no snow and $C_{\rm snow}$ when snow fills the active layer completely. 

For the insulating snow below and the first soil layer, the heat flow must pass through both media in series. Writing the original first soil layer thickness as $h_1$, the model uses for this combined layer

$$
h_1^*=h_1+h_{\rm ins}.
$$

The effective conductivity follows as

$$
K_1^*=
\frac{K_{\rm soil}K_{\rm snow}(h_1+h_{\rm ins})}
{K_{\rm snow}h_1+K_{\rm soil}h_{\rm ins}}.
\tag{4.2.6}
$$

The same combined layer also uses an effective volumetric heat capacity,

$$
C_1^*=
\frac{C_{\rm soil}C_{\rm snow}(h_1+h_{\rm ins})}
{C_{\rm soil}h_{\rm ins}+C_{\rm snow}h_1}.
$$

There are two "upper limits" here that are easy to confuse: the physical snow depth used in the thermal reconstruction is capped at $1\ \mathrm{m}$, while the snow reservoir itself has a default upper limit of $5\ \mathrm{m}$ **water equivalent**. The former bounds the thermal thickness of the effective medium; the latter bounds the water stored in the state variable. They are not the same parameter.

Three cases are the most intuitive check of these formulas:

| Snow condition | Surface active layer $C_m$ | First combined layer $(h_1^*,K_1^*,C_1^*)$ |
|---|---|---|
| No snow, $h_s=0$ | Pure soil | The original first soil layer |
| Thin snow, $0<h_s<h_m$ | Snow–soil blend | Still the original first soil layer |
| Thick snow, $h_s>h_m$ | Pure snow | First soil layer plus insulating snow |

Even thin snow can noticeably weaken the heat exchange between the surface and the soil through its large thermal resistance. This produces an important climate effect: **during a cold outbreak the snow surface can cool rapidly while the soil beneath remains insulated; when the weather warms, the deep soil cannot immediately deliver heat to the snow surface either.**

Thus thin snow first changes the heat capacity of the surface active layer; once the snow is thicker than $0.20\ {\rm m}$, the excess additionally raises the thermal resistance between the surface and the first soil layer.

## 5. Five Soil Layers

### 5.1 Continuous Governing Equation

The soil column at each land grid point considers vertical heat conduction only (Carslaw & Jaeger, 1959):

$$
C(z)\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left(K(z)\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right).
\tag{4.2.7}
$$

Neglecting horizontal soil heat conduction is a reasonable scale approximation: an atmospheric grid point is hundreds of kilometers wide, whereas diurnal and seasonal soil temperature variations propagate mainly in the vertical direction.

The default layer thicknesses are

$$
h_k=(0.4,\ 0.8,\ 1.6,\ 3.2,\ 6.4)\ \mathrm{m}.
\tag{4.2.8}
$$

The thickness doubles with depth, so the shallow layers resolve the faster temperature variations while the deep layers provide a longer thermal memory with fewer degrees of freedom. The bottom boundary has zero heat flux, which amounts to assuming that the deeper ground exchanges no heat with the modeled soil column during the simulation period.

### 5.2 Heat Flux Between Layers

The conductivity and thickness of two adjacent layers may differ. Writing the thermal resistances between the two layer centers in series gives the interface conductivity

$$
\Gamma_{k+1/2}
=\frac{2K_kK_{k+1}}
{K_kh_{k+1}+K_{k+1}h_k}.
\tag{4.2.9}
$$

The factor 2 in (4.2.9) comes from the "half-layer thickness".

The discrete energy conservation equation is

$$
C_kh_k\frac{T_k^{n+1}-T_k^n}{\Delta t}
=G_{k-1/2}^{n+1}-G_{k+1/2}^{n+1}.
\tag{4.2.10}
$$

The same interface flux is a loss for the layer above and a gain for the layer below, so internal conduction neither creates nor destroys energy. All new layer temperatures appear in (4.2.10) simultaneously, making the five layer equations a tridiagonal linear system. The model solves it implicitly, so it is not subject to the strict time-step limit of explicit heat diffusion; even with thin, highly conductive shallow layers it can advance stably with the same time step as the atmosphere.

The tridiagonal system is written out step by step below. To simplify the notation, first define

$$
\Lambda_k\equiv\frac{C_kh_k}{\Delta t}.
$$

Both $\Lambda_k$ and $\Gamma_{k\pm1/2}$ have units of
$\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$, so they can serve directly as coefficients in the same linear system for temperature. For an interior layer $2\le k\le N-1$ (here $N=5$),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Lambda_k(T_k^{n+1}-T_k^n)
={}&\Gamma_{k-1/2}(T_{k-1}^{n+1}-T_k^{n+1})\\
&-\Gamma_{k+1/2}(T_k^{n+1}-T_{k+1}^{n+1}).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term on the right-hand side is the heat entering layer $k$ through its upper interface and the second is the heat leaving layer $k$ through its lower interface. Expanding the right-hand side gives

$$
\Lambda_kT_k^{n+1}-\Lambda_kT_k^n
=\Gamma_{k-1/2}T_{k-1}^{n+1}
-(\Gamma_{k-1/2}+\Gamma_{k+1/2})T_k^{n+1}
+\Gamma_{k+1/2}T_{k+1}^{n+1}.
$$

Moving all new-time temperatures to the left-hand side and keeping the known old-time temperatures on the right gives

$$
-\Gamma_{k-1/2}T_{k-1}^{n+1}
+(\Lambda_k+\Gamma_{k-1/2}+\Gamma_{k+1/2})T_k^{n+1}
-\Gamma_{k+1/2}T_{k+1}^{n+1}
=\Lambda_kT_k^n.
$$

This is exactly the tridiagonal form

$$
-a_kT_{k-1}^{n+1}+b_kT_k^{n+1}-c_kT_{k+1}^{n+1}=d_k.
$$

If the known heat flux from the surface into the soil is written as $G\equiv G_{1/2}$ and the bottom boundary uses $G_{N+1/2}=0$, the coefficients for all layers can be written uniformly as

$$
a_k=
\begin{cases}
0, & k=1,\\
\Gamma_{k-1/2}, & 2\le k\le N,
\end{cases}
\qquad
c_k=
\begin{cases}
\Gamma_{k+1/2}, & 1\le k\le N-1,\\
0, & k=N,
\end{cases}
$$

$$
b_k=\Lambda_k+a_k+c_k,
\qquad
d_k=\Lambda_kT_k^n+
\begin{cases}
G, & k=1,\\
0, & 2\le k\le N.
\end{cases}
$$

The equations for the top layer, an interior layer and the bottom layer are therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\Lambda_1+\Gamma_{3/2})T_1^{n+1}
-\Gamma_{3/2}T_2^{n+1}
&=\Lambda_1T_1^n+G,\\[3pt]
-\Gamma_{k-1/2}T_{k-1}^{n+1}
+(\Lambda_k+\Gamma_{k-1/2}+\Gamma_{k+1/2})T_k^{n+1}
-\Gamma_{k+1/2}T_{k+1}^{n+1}
&=\Lambda_kT_k^n,
\qquad 2\le k\le N-1,\\[3pt]
-\Gamma_{N-1/2}T_{N-1}^{n+1}
+(\Lambda_N+\Gamma_{N-1/2})T_N^{n+1}
&=\Lambda_NT_N^n.
\end{aligned}
$$

The system is solved with a direct $O(N)$ elimination of Thomas type (the current implementation calls LAPACK `SPTSV`, `src/math_tools/linear_algebra.c`; see Anderson et al., 1999), which returns all five $T_k^{n+1}$ at once.

### 5.3 Glacier Points

Glacier points replace the soil conductivity and heat capacity with the values for ice and constrain every layer temperature not to exceed the melting point:

$$
T_k\le T_m.
\tag{4.2.11}
$$

This treats a glacier as an ice column that cannot keep warming above the melting point in its solid interior. It is not a full glacier dynamics model: there are no ice flow, ice formation or mass balance equations, but the ice surface thermal properties and the high-albedo boundary are retained.

## 6. Snow Mass and the Latent Heat of Melt

### 6.1 Snowfall First Enters the Snow Reservoir

Before any melting occurs, the snow water equivalent satisfies

$$
\frac{dS}{dt}=P_s+E_s,
\tag{4.2.12}
$$

where $E_s$ is the snow-surface evaporation or sublimation flux apportioned to the snow reservoir from the unified surface evaporation flux $E$ when snow exists at the beginning of the time step; by the sign convention of this chapter it is usually negative.
The discrete update is followed by the constraint $S\ge0$, which prevents strong sublimation from making the snow water equivalent negative.

Only the part of the total precipitation that does not remain in the snow reservoir enters the soil water:

$$
F_W=E+P_r+P_s-\frac{dS}{dt}.
\tag{4.2.13}
$$

This form performs the rain–snow partitioning automatically:

- newly fallen solid precipitation that becomes snow cancels in (4.2.13);
- rain enters the soil directly;
- evaporation or sublimation is removed from the corresponding reservoir through the negative $E$.

### 6.2 Temperature Cannot Pass the Melting Point Without Paying Latent Heat

If (4.2.5) predicts $T_s^{n+1}>T_m$ while snow is present, the model first clamps the snow surface temperature to the melting point:

$$
T_s^{n+1}=T_m.
\tag{4.2.14}
$$

The surplus energy that would otherwise have heated the snow surface above the melting point is diagnosed as the melt energy $Q_m$. Since

$$
L_f=L_s-L_v,
\tag{4.2.15}
$$

that is, the latent heat of fusion equals the latent heat of sublimation minus the latent heat of vaporization, the melt rate is

$$
\dot S_{\rm melt}
=\min\left(
\frac{Q_m}{\rho_wL_f},
\frac{S}{\Delta t}
\right).
\tag{4.2.16}
$$

The second upper bound ensures that at most the currently available snow melts in one time step. The meltwater enters the soil water flux of (4.2.13), and $Q_m$ is subtracted from the surface energy budget. The disappearance of snow therefore satisfies both water conservation and the energy conservation of the phase change.

!!! note "Why the surface temperature is recomputed after all the snow has melted"

    If this step's energy is enough to melt all the snow, the energy actually used for the phase change only extends as far as "all melted"; the remaining energy should still heat the snow-free surface. The model therefore re-solves $T_s$ after the snow reservoir reaches zero, using the energy left after subtracting the actual latent heat of the melted snow, so that excess energy is not mistakenly left in a snow layer that no longer exists.

### 6.3 The Snow Upper Limit

After melting and soil heat conduction, the code also checks $S>S_{\max}$. Snow water equivalent above the limit is removed from the reservoir, counted as meltwater, and the corresponding latent heat is taken from the surface-layer heat content, which lowers $T_s$. This branch mainly prevents the simplified single-layer snow reservoir from growing without bound. It applies to the water equivalent; it is unrelated to the $1\ \mathrm{m}$ cap on the physical snow depth used for the thermal reconstruction of Section 4.

## 7. The Soil Water Bucket: A Minimal Land Hydrology with Capacity Truncation

### 7.1 Single-Reservoir Approximation

PlaSiC does not resolve infiltration into different soil layers; it compresses the available water of the whole soil column into a single reservoir:

$$
\frac{dW}{dt}=F_W,
\qquad
0\le W\le W_{\max},
\tag{4.2.17}
$$

where $W_{\max}$ is the field capacity limit of the grid point. Ignoring the bounds first gives the candidate value

$$
W^*=W^n+\Delta t F_W,
\tag{4.2.18}
$$

and the candidate is then clipped to the range allowed by the bucket, with the excess water diagnosed as

$$
\Delta W_{\rm excess}=\max(0,W^*-W_{\max}),
\qquad
W^{n+1}=\min[W_{\max},\max(0,W^*)].
\tag{4.2.19}
$$

This is the bucket model (Manabe, 1969) of the current implementation. Water above $W_{\max}$ does not enter a new state variable and is not passed to the sea ice or ocean modules; it simply leaves the model system. The implementation therefore retains the land–atmosphere feedback of "how much soil water is still available for evaporation", but it does not represent full global freshwater conservation, nor where the removed water goes afterwards. It also cannot represent different root depths or capillary rise.

### 7.2 How Soil Moisture Limits Evaporation

Soil water does not modify the air humidity directly. Instead it first builds the surface water availability factor

$$
\beta=
\min\left(1,\frac{W}{f_wW_{\max}}\right),
\tag{4.2.20}
$$

with the default $f_w=0.4$. Hence

- when $W\ge0.4W_{\max}$, $\beta=1$ and evaporation is not limited by the water source;
- as the soil keeps drying, $\beta$ decreases approximately linearly with $W$;
- with snow or a glacier, $\beta=1$ and the surface moisture supply is controlled by snow and ice rather than by soil water.

The turbulent exchange of the next atmosphere step can respond to the reduced $\beta$, giving

$$
W\downarrow
\ \Longrightarrow\ 
\beta\downarrow
\ \Longrightarrow\ 
|E|\downarrow
\ \Longrightarrow\ 
|LE|\downarrow.
\tag{4.2.21}
$$

When less of the available energy goes into latent heat, more of it tends to go into sensible heat and surface warming. This is the most important soil moisture–temperature feedback in the simplified land surface.

### 7.3 What the Prescribed-Mode Switches Change

The two switches are independent:

- if prognostic temperature is turned off, $T_s$ is taken directly from the monthly temperature climatology, and this step no longer solves the surface energy balance, snow or the five-layer soil heat conduction; precipitation and evaporation then form the soil water flux directly;
- if prognostic water is turned off, $W$ is taken directly from the interpolated monthly field, the bucket is not updated with this step's net water flux, and capacity truncation is not applied;
- by default both switches are on, so the full path computes heat, snow and soil water together.

This explains why the flow chart draws an independent decision on the temperature path and on the water path, rather than controlling the whole land module with a single switch.

## 8. How the Snow–Albedo Feedback Enters the Radiation

Without snow, the surface uses the background albedo $\alpha_b$ interpolated by month. With snow, a snow albedo is first constructed from the snow surface temperature (snow–albedo feedback: Budyko, 1969; Sellers, 1969; Robock, 1980; Wiscombe & Warren, 1980):

$$
\alpha_s(T_s)=
\operatorname{clip}
\left[
\alpha_{\max}
-(\alpha_{\max}-\alpha_{\min})
\frac{T_s-263.16}{T_m-263.16},
\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max}
\right].
\tag{4.2.22}
$$

The defaults are $\alpha_{\min}=0.4$ and $\alpha_{\max}=0.8$. Cold snow approaches the maximum albedo; as the snow temperature approaches the melting point the albedo decreases, representing the fact that wet and aged snow is usually darker.

The snow amount is blended with the background surface through a smooth cover function:

$$
\alpha
=\alpha_b+(\alpha_s-\alpha_b)
\frac{S}{S+S_0},
\qquad S_0=0.01\ \mathrm{m}.
\tag{4.2.23}
$$

When $S\ll S_0$ the grid point is still dominated by the background surface; when $S\gg S_0$ the albedo approaches the pure snow value. This avoids the discontinuous jump in which "a very thin new snow layer instantly turns a whole atmospheric grid point into pure snow albedo".

The closed loop of the snow–albedo feedback is therefore

$$
S\uparrow
\Rightarrow \alpha\uparrow
\Rightarrow (1-\alpha)S_{\downarrow}\downarrow
\Rightarrow T_s\downarrow
\Rightarrow \text{melt slows down}.
\tag{4.2.24}
$$

At glacier points this diagnosis is overridden: they use a higher albedo range and are always treated as surfaces with an abundant moisture supply.

## 9. How the Land Becomes the Lower Boundary of the Atmosphere

After updating its internal states, the land module does not modify the atmospheric temperature or water vapor directly; instead it prepares four kinds of boundary quantities for the atmosphere.

### 9.1 Temperature and Saturation Specific Humidity

The surface temperature becomes the lower boundary for atmospheric turbulence and longwave radiation. The surface saturation vapor pressure uses an exponential empirical formula in temperature (coefficients after Lowe, 1977),

$$
e_s(T_s)=a_1
\exp\left[
a_2\frac{T_s-T_m}{T_s-a_4}
\right],
\tag{4.2.25}
$$

with the same coefficients $a_1$, $a_2$ and $a_4$ as the atmospheric saturation formula. It is then converted to a saturation specific humidity using the surface pressure $p_s$:

$$
q_s^*(T_s,p_s)
=\frac{\varepsilon e_s}
{p_s-(1-\varepsilon)e_s},
\qquad
\varepsilon=\frac{R_d}{R_v}.
\tag{4.2.26}
$$

Here $q_s^*$ is the humidity the surface could maintain **if water were plentiful**. The actual evaporation is still limited by $\beta$ from (4.2.20), so the thermal condition and the water source condition are clearly separated: $T_s$ sets the saturation value, and $W$ determines whether that saturation value can be reached.

### 9.2 Albedo and Roughness

The albedo from (4.2.23) is returned to the radiation module. The roughness comes from the prescribed land roughness field, which controls the turbulent exchange coefficients for momentum, sensible heat and water vapor. In the code it is set once from the climatology when the exchange fields are initialized, and it is not updated again during the integration. The current simplified land surface has no seasonal growth of a vegetation canopy, so roughness does not evolve online with the vegetation state.

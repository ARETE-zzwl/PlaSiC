# 4.5 Slab Ocean

PlaSiC's slab ocean is forced to a single mixed layer with a prescribed depth and finite heat capacity. It provides the atmosphere-facing sea surface temperature (SST). It has no prognostic current, salinity, stratification, or deep-ocean storage, and vertical and horizontal diffusion are disabled: each ocean point only exchanges heat with the atmosphere, the sea ice, and the deep ocean. The standard configuration also uses a three-dimensional ocean to supply a prognostic deep-ocean heat flux; it is documented in [4.6 Three-Dimensional Ocean](4.6-three-dimensional-ocean.md).

## 1. State and heat capacity

The slab stores one temperature $T_o$ and one mixed-layer depth $H$ at each ocean grid point. Its heat capacity per unit area is

$$
 C_o=\rho_o c_{p,o}H.
$$

The default depth is $H=50\ \mathrm{m}$, with $\rho_o=1030\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $c_{p,o}=4180\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. Thus $C_o\approx2.15\times10^8\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$.

`ocean_initialize_parameters()` sets the same $H$ at every horizontal point and divides one solar day into `ntspd` ocean time steps:

$$
\Delta t_{mix}=\frac{T_{sol}}{N_{tspd}}.
$$

The SST and mixed-layer depth are stored as horizontal fields. Monthly SST and sea-ice climatologies are linearly interpolated before a coupling step.

## 2. Local mixed-layer budget

Let $Q_a$ be the net atmospheric heat flux entering the sea-surface system and $Q_{deep}$ an additional heat flux from the deep ocean or a prescribed Q-flux. For an ice-free ocean point,

$$
 C_o\frac{dT_o}{dt}=Q_a+Q_{deep}.
$$

The explicit update used by `ocean_mksst_begin()` is

$$
 T_o^*=T_o^n+\frac{\Delta t_{mix}}{\rho_o c_{p,o}H}
 (Q_a+Q_{deep}).
$$

A positive flux warms the slab. For example, $100\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ applied for one day changes a 50 m slab by about $0.040\ \mathrm{K}$. A deeper mixed layer responds more slowly because the temperature increment is proportional to $H^{-1}$.

The slab does not calculate horizontal transport, currents, or diffusion. A prescribed Q-flux can represent the part of the climatological heat budget not explained by local atmospheric fluxes:

$$
 Q_{deep}^{clim}=C_o\left\langle\frac{dT_o}{dt}\right\rangle_{clim}
 -\left\langle Q_a\right\rangle_{clim}.
$$

When the three-dimensional ocean is enabled, its top-layer budget supplies $Q_{deep}$ instead of a fixed correction.

## 3. Freezing-point energy handoff

The trial value $T_o^*$ must not fall below the saline-water freezing point $T_f=271.25\ \mathrm{K}$. The model does not simply clip the temperature and discard the difference. If $T_o^*<T_f$, it sets $T_o=T_f$ and converts the missing sensible heat into an ocean-to-ice flux,

$$
 Q_{o\to i}=\frac{C_o}{\Delta t_{mix}}(T_o^*-T_f).
$$

This quantity is negative when the ocean loses heat and therefore forms ice. It is passed to the ice ledger, where

$$
 \Delta h_i=-\frac{Q_{o\to i}\Delta t_{mix}}{\rho_iL_i}>0.
$$

When ice is present, the ocean does not freely cool below $T_f$. The remaining heat exchange is represented by the ice flux returned through `ocean_copy_outputs()`; the sign is reversed at that interface so that the ocean and ice use their own consistent conventions.

The ocean kernel uses double precision for the temporary SST during a step and writes the final value back to the single-precision state.

## 4. Optional Newtonian restoring

With `ocean.flux_correction = 1`, `ocean_finish_step()` computes an online restoring flux toward the interpolated climatological SST $T_{clim}$:

$$
 Q_R=\frac{\rho_o c_{p,o}H}{\tau_{aunc}}
 (T_{clim}-T_o).
$$

Here `taunc` is converted from days to model time during initialization. Positive $Q_R$ warms the slab and negative $Q_R$ cools it. The restoring flux is applied only in the direction that moves SST toward its target.

If the model is run without a prognostic ocean, the climatological SST is assigned directly. With a prognostic ocean and no restoring, the slab evolves only from the atmospheric and deep-ocean heat budgets.

## 5. Coupling with sea ice

At a sea-ice coupling time, the relevant order is:

1. the atmosphere provides the averaged heat flux;
2. the ice module computes the surface temperature, snow melt, and conductive flux;
3. the slab applies the ocean heat budget and enforces $T_f$;
4. the ice module consumes the returned $Q_{o\to i}$ and updates thickness;
5. the sea interface sends $T_i$, $T_o$, cover, thickness, and snow back to the atmosphere.

The freezing-point correction is therefore an energy transfer, not an arbitrary temperature limiter. If all ice melts, the ice kernel returns the unused melting energy to the ocean so that the slab can warm in the same step.

## 6. Scope and connection to the deep ocean

The slab is designed to provide thermal inertia at low computational cost. By itself it cannot represent ocean circulation, salinity-driven density changes, vertical stratification, upwelling, long-term deep heat storage, or vertical and horizontal diffusion. The three-dimensional module adds the circulation, convection and diffusion processes below the 50 m top layer and returns a prognostic deep-ocean heat flux to this slab. See [4.6 Three-Dimensional Ocean](4.6-three-dimensional-ocean.md).

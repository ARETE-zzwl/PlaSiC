# 3.4 Radiation Parameterization { #radiation-parameterization }

A radiation scheme does not track individual photons. It estimates the upward and downward fluxes at
each layer interface from the solar geometry, atmospheric state, clouds, and surface properties.
PlaSiC uses separate shortwave and longwave branches, but both ultimately convert layer energy
convergence into a radiative temperature tendency. The clear-sky shortwave branch follows Lacis
and Hansen (1974), the cloud optics follow Stephens (1978, 1984) and Stephens et al. (1984), and the
longwave broadband transmissivities follow Sasamori (1968).


## 0. First the Big Picture: How One Radiation Calculation Is Completed

On a first reading, follow Figure 3.4.1 from top to bottom. The shortwave and longwave branches use
different spectral approximations, then meet when their flux divergences are combined.

![PlaSiC radiation parameterization algorithm flow](<../assets/images/radiation-parameterization-flow.svg>)

Figure 3.4.1. Overall computational order of the radiation parameterization.


!!! tip "Distinguish three easily confused quantities first"

    - **Mass mixing ratio** $q_X$: how much absorber one kilogram of moist air contains; a local
      state variable. H$_2$O and O$_3$ use this form, whereas the CO$_2$ field is supplied in ppmv
      and converted internally;
    - **Vertical column amount** $u_X$: the absorber mass per unit area obtained by integrating
      upward or downward from a given height;
    - **Effective path amount** $x$: the column amount multiplied by slant-path, pressure, or
      temperature corrections; this is the actual argument of the empirical absorption function.

    The radiation scheme never feeds $q_X$ directly into an absorptance. It proceeds through
    $q_X\rightarrow u_X\rightarrow x$.

## 1. Basic Setup

The model uses $\sigma$ coordinates. Layer $l$ of thickness $\Delta\sigma_l$ has

$$
\Delta p_l=p_s\Delta\sigma_l,
\qquad
\Delta m_l=\frac{\Delta p_l}{g}
             =\frac{p_s\Delta\sigma_l}{g}.
\tag{3.4.1}
$$

Here $\Delta m_l$ is the mass of air per unit horizontal area. Hence if an absorber has mass mixing
ratio $q_X$, then $q_X\Delta p/g$ is the absorber mass per unit area in that layer. Although the
"effective path amounts" in the shortwave and longwave schemes use different empirical units, both
start from Eq. (3.4.1) and then apply pressure, temperature, and slant-path corrections.

### 1.1 Reflection, Transmission, and Absorption in a Layer Must Close

Imagine a beam of unit energy entering a layer from above. If a fraction $R_l$ is reflected, a
fraction $T_l$ passes through, and a fraction $A_l$ is absorbed, energy conservation requires

$$
R_l+T_l+A_l=1.
\tag{3.4.2}
$$

Knowing any two of the three determines the third. For example, the shortwave cloud scheme computes
$R_l$ and $T_l$ first and then obtains absorption as $A_l=1-R_l-T_l$; the longwave scheme usually
computes a transmittance $\mathcal T$ first and invokes Kirchhoff's law to interpret
$1-\mathcal T$ as both absorptance and emissivity.

### 1.2 A Single Convention: Net Downward Positive

Let $D_l$ be the magnitude of the downward flux and $U_l$ the magnitude of the upward flux at
interface $l$. The net downward flux is

$$
F_l=D_l-U_l.
\tag{3.4.3}
$$

The implementation stores the upward component as a negative number, so it can add the two signed
components directly. This document uses the more intuitive positive magnitudes $D$ and $U$.

The radiative temperature tendency of layer $l$ is

$$
\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\right)_{\!rad,l}
=-
\frac{g}{c_p\,p_s\Delta\sigma_l\,(1+a_vq_l)}
\left(F_{l+1}-F_l\right),
\tag{3.4.4}
$$

Here $c_p$ is the heat capacity of dry air and $a_v$ is the water-vapor correction
($a_v=c_{pv}/c_{pd}-1$ in the implementation). The sign of the tendency follows directly:

- if $F_{l+1}<F_l$, the energy propagating downward decreases within the layer and the difference is
  absorbed there, giving a positive tendency;
- if $F_{l+1}>F_l$, the layer exports more radiative energy than it receives, giving a negative
  tendency.

Shortwave, longwave, and total radiation each use the same Eq. (3.4.4), so their temperature
tendencies add up exactly.

## 2. Solar Geometry

### 2.1 Zenith Angle

At latitude $\phi$, solar declination $\delta$, and hour angle $h$, the cosine of the solar zenith
angle is

$$
\mu_0=\cos\theta_0
=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta\cos h.
\tag{3.4.5}
$$

Figures 3.4.2 and 3.4.3 evaluate Eq. (3.4.5) at the two solstices on the T85 Gaussian grid
($256\times128$), with the globe split into two opposite hemispheres so that every grid point is
visible exactly once.

![Cosine of the solar zenith angle at the June solstice](<../assets/images/radiation-zenith-angle-june-solstice.png>){: style="display: block; margin: 0.4rem auto 0.6rem;" }

Figure 3.4.2. Cosine of the solar zenith angle on the T85 grid at the June solstice (21 June,
00:00 UTC, $\delta=+23.68^\circ$), drawn as two opposite hemispheres. The white curve is the
terminator, $\mu_0=0$. **a**, Hemisphere centred on $90^\circ\mathrm W$; **b**, hemisphere centred on
$90^\circ\mathrm E$. Grid points with the Sun below the horizon carry $\mu_0=0$, so the night side of
each disk is uniformly dark. At 00:00 UTC the subsolar meridian is the dateline, so the terminator
crosses the equator at $90^\circ\mathrm E$ and $90^\circ\mathrm W$ and projects to a straight line
through the centre of both disks: each disk is half lit, and the two disks together show the whole
sphere.

![Cosine of the solar zenith angle at the December solstice](<../assets/images/radiation-zenith-angle-december-solstice.png>){: style="display: block; margin: 0.4rem auto 0.6rem;" }

Figure 3.4.3. The same field at the December solstice (21 December, 12:00 UTC,
$\delta=-23.70^\circ$). At 12:00 UTC the subsolar meridian is Greenwich, so the terminator again
crosses the equator at $90^\circ\mathrm E$ and $90^\circ\mathrm W$, but the illuminated side swaps:
the north polar cap that stayed sunlit in Figure 3.4.2 now sits in polar night, while the brightest
shading moves onto the rim at $23.7^\circ\mathrm S$ on the $0^\circ$ meridian of panel **a**.

To keep the plane-parallel approximation from producing an infinite path at sunrise and sunset, the scheme applies a finite spherical-atmosphere magnification factor following Rodgers (1967):

$$
M(\mu_0)=\frac{35}{\sqrt{1+1224\mu_0^2}}.
\tag{3.4.6}
$$

For a plane-parallel atmosphere the simplest slant-path factor is $1/\mu_0$. At the zenith
$\mu_0=1$ and the path is shortest; as the Sun lowers, $1/\mu_0$ grows. But $1/\mu_0$ diverges as
$\mu_0\rightarrow0$, whereas the path through a real spherical atmosphere is finite. Equation
(3.4.6) replaces this unphysical divergence with a finite upper bound:

$$
M(1)=1,
\qquad
M(0)=35.
\tag{3.4.7}
$$

It therefore reproduces the plane-parallel result when the Sun is high and limits the longest
optical path to about 35 vertical columns near the horizon.

The scheme resolves the diurnal cycle explicitly. At every radiation time step the solar
declination is obtained from the current date, and the local $\mu_0$ of each grid point from the
current time, latitude, and longitude. Every longitude uses its instantaneous value to compute
incident energy and optical path; at grid points where the Sun is below the horizon the code sets
$\mu_0=0$, so the night side receives no shortwave radiation (these are the uniformly dark regions of
Figures 3.4.2 and 3.4.3). The shortwave kernel also skips a
column when the resulting top-of-atmosphere flux is below its $10^{-6}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ threshold.

### 2.2 Top-of-Atmosphere Incident Flux

The solar flux on a horizontal surface at the top of the atmosphere is written as

$$
F_{0,b}=w_bS_0d_r\mu_0,
\tag{3.4.8}
$$

where the solar spectrum has been compressed into two bands and $w_b$ distributes the total energy
among them (Lacis & Hansen, 1974).

| Spectral band | Energy weight $w_b$ | Main processes |
|---|---:|---|
| Visible and ultraviolet, $\lambda<0.75\ \mu\mathrm m$ | 0.517 | Ozone absorption, Rayleigh scattering, cloud scattering |
| Near infrared, $\lambda>0.75\ \mu\mathrm m$ | 0.483 | Water vapor absorption, cloud scattering and cloud absorption |

Aerosol absorption and scattering are not included in the current scheme. The point of the two-band
approximation is to retain the contrast between visible and near-infrared radiation in their main
absorbers and in cloud absorption properties, without resolving thousands of spectral lines.

## 3. Shortwave: Turning Every Layer into a Reflection–Transmission System

For each spectral band and each atmospheric layer the scheme constructs four quantities:

$$
R_l,\quad T_l,\quad R_l^*,\quad T_l^*,
\tag{3.4.9}
$$

where the starred quantities refer to illumination from below and the unstarred ones to illumination
from above. Direction matters because direct sunlight and the diffuse light returned by the surface
and clouds have different effective paths and cloud-scattering properties.

### 3.1 Clear-Sky Ozone Absorption: First Spectral Band

The ozone absorptance function follows Lacis and Hansen (1974):

$$
A_{O_3}(x)=
\frac{0.02118x}{1+0.042x+0.000323x^2}
+\frac{1.082x}{(1+138.6x)^{0.805}}
+\frac{0.0658x}{1+(103.6x)^3}.
\tag{3.4.10}
$$

Here $x$ is not a local mixing ratio but the ozone column amount integrated along the optical path.
The direct beam uses $M$ from Eq. (3.4.6); light returned from below is diffuse and uses the fixed
diffuse magnification factor

$$
M_d=1.9.
\tag{3.4.11}
$$

In the code the magnification applied to each layer's ozone increment is cloud-aware: where the
atmosphere above the layer is still clear, the increment is multiplied by $M(\mu_0)$; where clouds
have already converted the beam into diffuse light, it is multiplied by $M_d$. The same factor
$M_d$ also multiplies the ozone below a layer when the upward diffuse transmittance is formed.

Since Eq. (3.4.10) gives the absorbed fraction relative to the total solar energy, within the first
band the scheme uses

$$
T_{O_3,1}=1-\frac{A_{O_3}(x)}{0.517}.
\tag{3.4.12}
$$

The transmittance of an individual layer is not obtained by subtracting its isolated absorptance
from one. Instead, the cumulative ozone amount from the top of the atmosphere down to that layer is
computed first, and the layer transmittance is recovered as the ratio of the transmittances of two
adjacent cumulative columns. In this way the nonlinearity of the empirical absorption function acts
correctly on the whole optical path.

This step is expanded below. Let the cumulative effective ozone amount from the top of the
atmosphere to the bottom of layer $l$ be

$$
X_l=\sum_{j=1}^{l}M_j\Delta u_{O_3,j},
\qquad X_0=0.
\tag{3.4.13}
$$

The empirical function gives the whole-path transmittance

$$
P(X_l)=1-\frac{A_{O_3}(X_l)}{0.517}.
\tag{3.4.14}
$$

The total transmittance from the top of the atmosphere to the bottom of layer $l$ also equals the
total transmittance of the first $l-1$ layers times the transmittance of layer $l$:

$$
P(X_l)=P(X_{l-1})T_{O_3,l}.
\tag{3.4.15}
$$

Hence the single-layer transmittance is not $1-A_{O_3}(\Delta u_l)$ but

$$
T_{O_3,l}=\frac{P(X_l)}{P(X_{l-1})}.
\tag{3.4.16}
$$

This distinction matters. Because $A_{O_3}$ is nonlinear, the same amount of ozone placed at the
end of a nearly saturated column adds less absorption than it would at the start of a thin column.
Equation (3.4.16) preserves this saturation effect.

### 3.2 Clear-Sky Water Vapor Absorption: Second Spectral Band

The near-infrared water vapor absorptance (Lacis & Hansen, 1974) is

$$
A_{H_2O}(y)=
\frac{2.9y}{(1+141.5y)^{0.635}+5.925y}.
\tag{3.4.17}
$$

The effective water vapor path comes from

$$
y(p_1,p_2)=
\frac{M}{g}
\int_{p_1}^{p_2}
0.1q
\left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right)
\left(\frac{T_0}{T}\right)^{1/2}dp,
\tag{3.4.18}
$$

with $p_0=10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$ and $T_0=273\ \mathrm K$. The direct part again uses $M(\mu_0)$; the
diffuse part uses

$$
M_d=1.66.
\tag{3.4.19}
$$

Within the second band the water vapor transmittance is

$$
T_{H_2O,2}=1-\frac{A_{H_2O}(y)}{0.483}.
\tag{3.4.20}
$$

In discrete layer $l$, Eq. (3.4.18) becomes

$$
\Delta y_l\approx
M_l\frac{0.1}{g}
q_l\left(\frac{\sigma_lp_s}{p_0}\right)
\left(\frac{T_0}{T_l}\right)^{1/2}
p_s\Delta\sigma_l.
\tag{3.4.21}
$$

This expression makes the order of operations explicit: first form the water vapor mass per unit
area $q_lp_s\Delta\sigma_l/g$, then multiply it by the pressure-broadening correction $p_l/p_0$, the
temperature correction $\sqrt{T_0/T_l}$, and the slant-path correction $M_l$. As for ozone, the
clear-sky fraction above each layer decides whether the increment is magnified by $M$ or by the
diffuse factor $M_d$, and the single-layer transmittance again follows from Eq. (3.4.16), i.e. the
ratio of the whole-path transmittances of adjacent cumulative paths.

### 3.3 Why Rayleigh Scattering Sits in the Lowest Layer Only

The current simplified scheme concentrates all molecular scattering of the column in the lowest
model layer. Following Lacis and Hansen (1974), for the direct beam the column reflectance is
approximated as

$$
R_R^{dir}=\frac{0.219}{1+0.816\mu_0},
\tag{3.4.22}
$$

and for diffuse light

$$
R_R^{dif}=0.144.
\tag{3.4.23}
$$

"Lowest layer" here means a computational layer index, not a physical height. Real Rayleigh
scattering is produced by air molecules, and its optical depth is distributed over the whole column
with the air density, increasing toward the ground; it is never confined to a single model layer.
This simplification folds the whole-column molecular scattering into one equivalent reflectance and
inserts it at the lowest layer of the two-stream recursion.

Consider first the spectral placement. Rayleigh scattering varies approximately as $\lambda^{-4}$,
so it matters in the visible and ultraviolet but is already weak in the near infrared. The scheme
includes Rayleigh scattering only in the first band ($\lambda<0.75\ \mu\mathrm m$) and not in the
second, consistent with the band table above.

Equation (3.4.22) describes the direct beam. Molecular scattering turns part of the direct sunlight
upward; the lower the Sun, the smaller $\mu_0$, the longer the slant path through the air, and the
stronger the scattering. Equation (3.4.23) applies to light that has already become diffuse, for
which directional information is lost and a fixed effective reflectance of $0.144$ is used.

Why place these two numbers in the lowest layer rather than in every layer? Because Eqs. (3.4.22)
and (3.4.23) are whole-column empirical reflectances, not the optical depth of one thin layer.
Distributing them over every layer would count a single column effect many times; placing them at
the top of the atmosphere would let reflection act before ozone, water vapor, and cloud absorption,
in the wrong physical order. Placing them at the lowest layer means that in the top-down sweep of
the two-stream recursion the direct beam first crosses all gas and cloud layers above, and only at
the bottom is the upward component produced by Rayleigh scattering inserted; that component is then
carried back up to the top of the atmosphere. The scattering acts as an equivalent scattering
boundary just above the surface, not as a source at a real altitude.

The corresponding code steps are as follows. In `rad_shortwave` the index of the lowest layer at
each horizontal grid point is

$$
lowest=h+N_h(N_{lev}-1),
\tag{3.4.24}
$$

where $h$ is the horizontal grid point, $N_h$ the total number of horizontal grid points, and
$N_{lev}$ the number of vertical layers, with layer numbering starting at 0. The code first sets
`zrcs` and `zrcsu` to zero in all layers and assigns values only at `lowest`:

$$
zrcs_{lowest}=
\left[
R_R^{dir} z_{cs}
+
R_R^{dif}\left(1-z_{cs}-C_{lowest}\right)
\right]
\times \iota_R,
\tag{3.4.25}
$$

$$
zrcsu_{lowest}=
R_R^{dif}\left(1-C_{lowest}\right)
\times \iota_R,
\tag{3.4.26}
$$

Here `zrcs` is the Rayleigh reflectance used when the first band is illuminated from above, `zrcsu`
the diffuse reflectance used when it is illuminated from below, $C_{lowest}$ the cloud cover of the
lowest layer, and $\iota_R$ the `rayleigh_scattering` switch (0 or 1).

$z_{cs}$ is the clear-sky fraction of the whole column, computed with random overlap:

$$
z_{cs}=\prod_{l=0}^{N_{lev}-1}\left(1-C_l\right).
\tag{3.4.27}
$$

Equation (3.4.25) partitions the downward radiation at the lowest layer into three mutually
exclusive cases:

| Conditions above the lowest layer | Area fraction | Which reflectance |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Entire column clear, direct beam reaches the lowest layer intact | $z_{cs}$ | $R_R^{dir}$ |
| At least one cloud above, but lowest layer clear, light already diffused by cloud | $1-z_{cs}-C_{lowest}$ | $R_R^{dif}$ |
| Lowest layer itself cloudy | $C_{lowest}$ | Not Rayleigh; handled by cloud reflectance |

### 3.4 Representing Clouds

The scheme uses a Stephens-type parameterization (Stephens, 1978; Stephens et al., 1984). The cloud liquid water path is

$$
W_L=1000\,q_l\frac{p_s\Delta\sigma}{g}
\qquad[\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}],
\tag{3.4.28}
$$

capped at $1000\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}$. The effective cloud optical depth is approximated as

$$
\tau_N=2\left[\log_{10}(W_L+1.5)\right]^{3.9}.
\tag{3.4.29}
$$

The offset $+1.5$ keeps the logarithm well defined for very thin clouds. It also means that the fit
has a small nonzero floor,
$\tau_N(0)=2[\log_{10}(1.5)]^{3.9}\approx2.29\times10^{-3}$, rather than tending exactly to zero.

Note the distinction: $q_l$ is only the local mass fraction of cloud water in the air, $W_L$ the
total cloud water in the whole layer, and $\tau_N$ how opaque that cloud water is to radiation. The
three are not interchangeable.

In the first band the cloud is treated as a non-absorbing scatterer:

$$
\beta_1=f_{b1}\sqrt{\mu},
\qquad
T_{c,1}=\frac{1}{1+\beta_1\tau_N/\mu},
\qquad
R_{c,1}=1-T_{c,1}.
\tag{3.4.30}
$$

Here $\beta_1$ is the **effective backscatter coefficient** of the first band. When sunlight enters
a cloud, the droplets scatter it in many directions: the part that continues downward contributes
to transmission, and the part turned back upward forms cloud reflection. A single number $\beta_1$
summarizes "how much of the scattering acts as upward reflection"; the larger $\beta_1$, the more
strongly a cloud of a given optical depth reflects sunlight.

The same expressions are evaluated twice. For the direct beam, set $\mu=\mu_0$; for diffuse light,
use the fixed diffusivity factor $\mu=0.5$. For downward radiation, the code area-weights the direct
and diffuse cloud properties. The direct part is weighted by the clear-sky fraction above the layer;
the remainder is treated as diffuse illumination. The upward (starred) properties always use the
diffuse values because the upward field contains no direct solar beam.

The default coefficient is

$$
f_{b1}=0.077.
\tag{3.4.31}
$$

For a very thin cloud, the limiting behavior is

$$
\tau_N\ll1: T_{c,1}\approx1,
\quad R_{c,1}\approx0,
\tag{3.4.32}
$$

$$
\tau_N\rightarrow\infty: T_{c,1}\rightarrow0,
\quad R_{c,1}\rightarrow1.
\tag{3.4.33}
$$

Because of the $+1.5$ offset, a zero-water cloud has a very small, not exactly zero, optical depth;
it is effectively transparent. An optically very thick non-absorbing cloud tends to total
reflection.

The second band includes both scattering and absorption. First define

$$
\beta_2=
\frac{f_{b2}\sqrt{\mu}}{\ln(3+0.1\tau_N)},
\qquad
\widetilde\omega_0=
\min\!\left[0.9999,
1-f_{o2}\mu^2\ln\!\left(\frac{1000}{\tau_N}\right)
\right],
\tag{3.4.34}
$$

where $f_{b2}$ and $f_{o2}$, like $f_{b1}$ in the first band, are dimensionless constants fixed for
the duration of a radiation calculation.

Thus $f_{b2}$ is the **tuning coefficient for effective backscatter** in the second band.
All else equal, a larger $f_{b2}$ raises the effective backscatter and
usually makes the cloud reflect more.

The coefficient $f_{o2}$ is the **tuning coefficient for cloud absorption strength** in the second
band. For the usual range $0<\tau_N<1000$, $\ln(1000/\tau_N)>0$, so a larger $f_{o2}$ gives a
smaller $\widetilde\omega_0$. Since $1-\widetilde\omega_0$ is the fraction of a single interaction
contributed by absorption, a larger $f_{o2}$ means stronger near-infrared cloud absorption. The
$\min(0.9999,\cdots)$ only keeps $\widetilde\omega_0$ slightly below one when absorption is very
weak, avoiding numerical singularities in the two-stream formulas that follow.

The default coefficients are

$$
f_{b2}=0.065,
\qquad
f_{o2}=0.0055.
$$

A compact mnemonic:

$$
f_{b2}\uparrow
\Longrightarrow
\beta_2\uparrow
\Longrightarrow
\text{stronger scattering reflection},
$$

$$
f_{o2}\uparrow
\Longrightarrow
\widetilde\omega_0\downarrow
\Longrightarrow
\text{stronger cloud absorption}.
\tag{3.4.35}
$$

Now let

$$
u^2=
\frac{1-\widetilde\omega_0+2\beta_2\widetilde\omega_0}
     {1-\widetilde\omega_0},
\tag{3.4.36}
$$

$$
\tau_{eff}=\frac{\tau_N}{\mu}
\sqrt{(1-\widetilde\omega_0)
\left(1-\widetilde\omega_0+2\beta_2\widetilde\omega_0\right)},
\tag{3.4.37}
$$

$$
\mathcal D=(u+1)^2e^{\tau_{eff}}-(u-1)^2e^{-\tau_{eff}}.
\tag{3.4.38}
$$

Then

$$
T_{c,2}=\frac{4u}{\mathcal D},
\qquad
R_{c,2}=\frac{(u^2-1)
(e^{\tau_{eff}}-e^{-\tau_{eff}})}{\mathcal D}.
\tag{3.4.39}
$$

These are the two-stream reflection and transmission; the remaining $1-R-T$ is cloud absorption. To
avoid overflow for very thick clouds, the code caps $\tau_{eff}$ at 25 before exponentiating.

### 3.5 How Cloud Cover Enters the Single-Layer Energy Budget

With cloud cover $C_l$, a grid cell is approximated as a clear part and a cloudy part (Slingo & Slingo, 1991). In the first
band, reflection is the sum of a Rayleigh contribution and the cloud contribution weighted by cloud
cover, while ozone absorption removes energy only from the clear-sky area. 

In the second band, clear-sky water vapor absorption and the cloudy part's $1-T_{c,2}$ are weighted separately by area. This yields $R_l$, $T_l$, $R_l^*$, $T_l^*$ satisfying single-layer energy closure.

Writing the area average in full makes the structure clearer. Let $T_g$ denote the gas-only
transmittance of the layer. In the first band the cloudy part only scatters while the clear part
absorbs through the ozone fit, so

$$
R_{l,1}=R_{R,l}+C_lR_{c,l,1},
\tag{3.4.40}
$$

$$
T_{l,1}=1-
(1-T_{g,l,1})(1-C_l)-R_{l,1}.
\tag{3.4.41}
$$

Equation (3.4.41) subtracts from unit energy first the ozone absorption on the clear-sky area and
then the reflection of the whole cell; the remainder is transmission.

In the second band the cloudy part can both scatter and absorb, so

$$
R_{l,2}=C_lR_{c,l,2},
\tag{3.4.42}
$$

$$
T_{l,2}=1-
(1-T_{g,l,2})(1-C_l)
-(1-T_{c,l,2})C_l.
\tag{3.4.43}
$$

The second term of Eq. (3.4.43) is the energy loss caused by clear-sky water vapor, and the third
term is the part of the cloudy area that does not penetrate the cloud. Finally
$A_{l,b}=1-R_{l,b}-T_{l,b}$ checks the single-layer energy closure of each band. For diffuse light
traveling upward, the identical area-average relations use the starred optical properties.

### 3.6 Surface Albedo Is the Shortwave Lower Boundary

Land and sea ice use an externally prescribed surface albedo. The direct albedo of ice-free ocean
varies with solar elevation following the ECHAM3 parameterization (Roeckner et al., 1992):

$$
\alpha_{ocn}^{dir}
=\min\!\left(\frac{0.05}{\mu_0+0.15},\ 0.15\right).
\tag{3.4.44}
$$

Specular reflection from the sea surface is stronger when the Sun is near the horizon and the albedo
is lower when the Sun is high. Diffuse light still uses the prescribed surface albedo. Land, sea
ice, and open ocean are combined with their respective area weights into the grid-point mean lower
boundary.

## 4. Shortwave Vertical Integration: How the Adding Method Counts Unlimited Reflections

### 4.1 Two-Layer Combination Formulas

Let the upper layer be $a$ and the lower layer $b$. For illumination from above, the reflectance and
transmittance of the combined layer are

$$
R_{ab}=R_a+
\frac{T_aR_bT_a^*}{1-R_a^*R_b},
\tag{3.4.45}
$$

$$
T_{ab}=\frac{T_aT_b}{1-R_a^*R_b}.
\tag{3.4.46}
$$

To see where Eq. (3.4.45) comes from, enumerate the paths that return to the top. Let the incident
energy be 1:

- energy reflected directly by the upper layer is $R_a$;
- energy transmitted through $a$, reflected by $b$, and transmitted back through $a$ is
  $T_aR_bT_a^*$;
- if it is reflected once more by the lower face of $a$ and once more by $b$, the contribution
  becomes $T_aR_b(R_a^*R_b)T_a^*$;
- one more round trip gives $T_aR_b(R_a^*R_b)^2T_a^*$.

The total reflectance is therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{ab}
&=R_a+T_aR_bT_a^*
\left[1+R_a^*R_b+(R_a^*R_b)^2+\cdots\right]\\
&=R_a+\frac{T_aR_bT_a^*}{1-R_a^*R_b},
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.4.47}
$$

which is Eq. (3.4.45). The shortest path transmitted to the bottom of the two layers is $T_aT_b$.
Before it actually crosses $b$, it may also bounce any number of times between the two layers, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{ab}
&=T_aT_b
\left[1+R_a^*R_b+(R_a^*R_b)^2+\cdots\right]\\
&=\frac{T_aT_b}{1-R_a^*R_b},
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.4.48}
$$

which is Eq. (3.4.46).

For illumination from below,

$$
R_{ab}^*=R_b^*+
\frac{T_b^*R_a^*T_b}{1-R_a^*R_b},
\qquad
T_{ab}^*=\frac{T_a^*T_b^*}{1-R_a^*R_b}.
\tag{3.4.49}
$$

The denominator comes from the geometric series

$$
1+R_a^*R_b+(R_a^*R_b)^2+\cdots
=\frac{1}{1-R_a^*R_b}.
\tag{3.4.50}
$$

The adding method therefore does not allow only one reflection per layer: the analytic denominator
sums every possible round trip between the two layers (Lacis & Hansen, 1974; see also Liou, 2002).

### 4.2 Two Sweeps and the Interface Fluxes

For an $L$-layer atmosphere, combine first from the top of the atmosphere downward to obtain
$T_{1:l}$ and $R_{1:l}^*$ for the whole atmosphere above each interface; then combine upward
starting from the surface albedo $R_s$ to obtain $R_{l+1:s}$ for the "atmosphere + surface" below
the interface.

At an arbitrary interface $l$, compress all atmosphere above into one combined layer and all
atmosphere and surface below into another. Let the incident flux at the top of the atmosphere be
normalized to 1. The downward flux reaching the interface has two parts: $T_{1:l}$, transmitted
directly through the upper system from the top of the atmosphere, and the part reflected by the
lower system and then reflected back downward by the upper system:

$$
D_l=T_{1:l}+R_{1:l}^*U_l.
\tag{3.4.51}
$$

The upward flux at the interface comes from reflection of $D_l$ by the lower system:

$$
U_l=R_{l+1:s}D_l.
\tag{3.4.52}
$$

Substituting Eq. (3.4.52) into Eq. (3.4.51),

$$
D_l=T_{1:l}+R_{1:l}^*R_{l+1:s}D_l.
\tag{3.4.53}
$$

Moving the terms containing $D_l$ to the left-hand side,

$$
D_l\left(1-R_{1:l}^*R_{l+1:s}\right)=T_{1:l}.
\tag{3.4.54}
$$

With the top-of-atmosphere incident flux normalized to 1, the interface fluxes are

$$
D_l=\frac{T_{1:l}}
{1-R_{1:l}^*R_{l+1:s}},
\tag{3.4.55}
$$

$$
U_l=\frac{T_{1:l}R_{l+1:s}}
{1-R_{1:l}^*R_{l+1:s}}.
\tag{3.4.56}
$$

The first and second bands perform this calculation separately; each result is multiplied by the
top-of-atmosphere band flux from Eq. (3.4.8) and the two are summed. The two bands share the same
geometrical structure and differ only in the single-layer absorption and scattering properties.

## 5. Longwave: The Broadband Emissivity Approach

Shortwave radiation has an external solar source, whereas longwave radiation is emitted by the
surface and by every atmospheric layer. The hemispheric blackbody flux is

$$
B(T)=\sigma_{SB}T^4,
\qquad
\sigma_{SB}=5.67\times10^{-8}
\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}.
\tag{3.4.57}
$$

Under the local thermodynamic equilibrium approximation, Kirchhoff's law gives "absorptance =
emissivity". Once the broadband transmittance $\mathcal T$ of a stretch of atmosphere is known, its
emission is $1-\mathcal T$.

### 5.1 Broadband Absorption by Water Vapor, Ozone, and Carbon Dioxide

The scheme uses the broadband emissivity method (Manabe & Möller, 1961; Rodgers, 1967; Sasamori,
1968; Katayama, 1972; Boer et al., 1984). Piecewise empirical fits replace the line-by-line
integration. The constants $C_w$, $C_c$, and $C_o$ below enforce
continuity between adjacent pieces; they are not additional tunable parameters.

The Beer–Lambert law would give an exponential transmittance $\mathcal T=e^{-ku}$. The real
atmosphere cannot be described by a single fixed absorption coefficient $k$, however. To see why,
start from a single absorption line.

#### 5.1.1 What an Absorption Line Is

A gas does not absorb equally strongly at all frequencies; it absorbs near a set of specific
frequencies, forming absorption peaks. These peaks are called **spectral lines**.

A line is not a needle of zero width either. Let the line-center frequency be $\nu_0$ and write the
absorption coefficient as $k_\nu$:

- near $\nu_0$, $k_\nu$ is largest; this part is the **line center**;
- away from $\nu_0$, $k_\nu$ decreases; the weaker but broader parts on either side form the
  **line wings**;
- between two lines absorption may be weak, creating a relatively transparent spectral window.

Even for a single gas, the absorption coefficient can therefore differ by many orders of magnitude
between frequencies. A real longwave band contains many neighboring lines of different strengths,
so the band cannot be treated as one "average line" with a fixed $k$.

At a fixed frequency $\nu$, define the optical depth

$$
\tau_\nu=k_\nu u,
$$

where $u$ is the effective absorber amount accumulated along the propagation path. The
transmittance and absorptance at that frequency are

$$
\mathcal T_\nu=e^{-\tau_\nu},
\qquad
A_\nu=1-\mathcal T_\nu=1-e^{-\tau_\nu}.
$$

Two limits are instructive.

**First, the optically thin limit, $\tau_\nu\ll1$.** Using the expansion of the exponential near
zero,

$$
e^{-\tau_\nu}
=1-\tau_\nu+\frac{\tau_\nu^2}{2}-\cdots,
$$

and dropping second- and higher-order terms gives

$$
A_\nu=1-e^{-\tau_\nu}\approx\tau_\nu=k_\nu u.
$$

When absorption is weak, doubling the absorber amount $u$ approximately doubles the absorptance.
Weak lines and the far wings of strong lines are usually in this regime. "Weak lines still grow
with the amount" refers to this approximately linear response.

**Second, the optically thick limit, $\tau_\nu\gg1$.** Here

$$
e^{-\tau_\nu}\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
A_\nu\longrightarrow1.
$$

Radiation at that frequency is almost entirely absorbed while crossing the gas. Further increases
in $u$ can only push $A_\nu$ from "very close to 1" to "even closer to 1". This state is called
**absorption saturation** at that frequency. Since $k_\nu$ is largest at the line center, strong
lines always saturate from the center outward.

The following numbers may help build intuition. Suppose the center of a strong line has $k_\nu=100$
and its wing has only $k_\nu=0.1$. When the path amount increases from $u=0.1$ to $u=1$:

| Spectral location | Change in $\tau_\nu$ | Change in $A_\nu$ | Physical meaning |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Line center | $10\rightarrow100$ | $0.99995\rightarrow$ almost $1$ | Already saturated; more gas has almost no effect |
| Line wing | $0.01\rightarrow0.1$ | $0.00995\rightarrow0.0952$ | Still optically thin; absorption grows appreciably |


### 5.2 Why Broadband Absorption Is No Longer a Simple Exponential

Integrating over a whole longwave band, the broadband absorptance can be written schematically as

$$
A_{\mathrm{band}}(u)
=\frac{\displaystyle\int W_\nu
\left[1-e^{-k_\nu u}\right]d\nu}
{\displaystyle\int W_\nu\,d\nu},
$$

where $W_\nu$ is the weight of frequency $\nu$ in the thermal flux. This is not an exponential
function but a weighted sum of exponential absorptions with many different $k_\nu$: strong line
centers saturate quickly, line wings grow more slowly, and weak lines may still grow nearly
linearly at larger $u$. The broadband absorptance therefore keeps increasing, but more and more
slowly.

Rather than evaluating tens of thousands of lines, the current scheme summarizes this behavior with
piecewise empirical functions: a power law for the rapid growth at small amounts and a logarithmic
form for the saturated regime where the marginal increase keeps shrinking.

When reading the formulas below, view them as one mapping:

$$
\text{cumulative effective path amount }u_X
\longrightarrow
\text{broadband absorptance of the whole path }A_X.
\tag{3.4.58}
$$


The water vapor absorptance is taken from Sasamori (1968):

$$
A_w(u)=
\begin{cases}
0.846(u+3.59\times10^{-5})^{0.243}-A_{w0}, & u\le0.01,\\[3pt]
0.24\log_{10}(u+0.01)+C_w, & u>0.01,
\end{cases}
\tag{3.4.59}
$$

optionally including water vapor continuum absorption,

$$
A_{cont}=1-e^{-k_{cont}u}.
\tag{3.4.60}
$$

With the continuum enabled, the total water vapor absorptance is

$$
A_w^{total}=\min\left(A_w+A_{cont},\ 1\right),
\tag{3.4.61}
$$

and $A_w$ in Eq. (3.4.68) below should be understood as this total, already including the continuum.

The ozone absorptance is

$$
A_o(u)=
\begin{cases}
0.209(u+7\times10^{-5})^{0.436}-A_{o0}, & u\le0.01,\\[3pt]
0.0212\log_{10}u+C_o, & u>0.01,
\end{cases}
\tag{3.4.62}
$$

and the carbon dioxide absorptance is

$$
A_c(u)=
\begin{cases}
0.0676(u+0.01022)^{0.421}-A_{c0}, & u\le1,\\[3pt]
0.0546\log_{10}u+C_c, & u>1.
\end{cases}
\tag{3.4.63}
$$

The offsets and continuity constants in these expressions are fixed entirely by the requirement that
the pieces join continuously:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{w0}&=0.846(3.59\times10^{-5})^{0.243},\\
C_w&=0.846(0.010+3.59\times10^{-5})^{0.243}
-A_{w0}-0.24\log_{10}(0.020),\\
A_{o0}&=0.209(7\times10^{-5})^{0.436},\\
C_o&=0.209(0.010+7\times10^{-5})^{0.436}
-A_{o0}-0.0212\log_{10}(0.010),\\
A_{c0}&=0.0676(0.01022)^{0.421},\\
C_c&=0.0676(1.01022)^{0.421}-A_{c0}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.4.64}
$$

The CO$_2$ 15 $\mu$m band overlaps the water vapor band, and simply adding the two absorptances
would double-count the spectral interval already absorbed by water vapor. The scheme corrects CO$_2$
with the residual water vapor transmittance in that band, $T_w^{15}$:

$$
T_w^{15}(u)=
\begin{cases}
1-0.832(u+0.0286)^{0.26}+H_0, & u\le2,\\[3pt]
\max\!\left[0,\ C_{15}-0.1196\ln(u-0.6931)\right], & u>2,
\end{cases}
\tag{3.4.65}
$$

where

$$
H_0=0.832(0.0286)^{0.26},
\tag{3.4.66}
$$

$$
C_{15}=1-0.832(2+0.0286)^{0.26}+H_0
+0.1196\ln(2-0.6931),
\tag{3.4.67}
$$

so the two pieces are continuous at $u=2$.

$$
\mathcal T_{clear}
=1-A_w-A_o-A_cT_w^{15}.
\tag{3.4.68}
$$

Equation (3.4.68) expresses that the more opaque the water vapor, the less of the same band CO$_2$
can additionally intercept. This is a low-cost approximation to spectral overlap in a broadband
scheme.

Subtracting these three parts from 1 gives the clear-sky transmittance. The implementation clips this
quantity to $[10^{-6},1-10^{-6}]$ before applying the cloud factor, preventing negative values and
the singular endpoints 0 and 1 in subsequent calculations.

### 5.3 Cloud Longwave Emissivity and Random Overlap

The longwave cloud emissivity is computed from the cloud liquid water path:

$$
\epsilon_{c,l}
=1-\exp(-1.66\,k_cW_{L,l}),
\tag{3.4.69}
$$

where $k_c$ is the cloud water mass absorption coefficient, of order
$0.1\ \mathrm{m^2\,g^{-1}}$ by default (Slingo & Slingo, 1991), and 1.66 is the diffuse path factor.
For thin clouds
$\epsilon_c$ grows approximately linearly with liquid water path; thick clouds approach a blackbody.

For a layer with cloud cover $C_l$, the cloud transmission factor after averaging over clear and
cloudy areas is

$$
\mathcal T_{cloud,l}
=(1-C_l)+C_l(1-\epsilon_{c,l})
=1-C_l\epsilon_{c,l}.
\tag{3.4.70}
$$

If a path crosses several cloud layers that are assumed to overlap randomly, then

$$
\mathcal T_{cloud}(i,j)
=\prod_{l=i}^{j}(1-C_l\epsilon_{c,l}).
\tag{3.4.71}
$$

The total path transmittance is

$$
\mathcal T(i,j)
=\mathcal T_{clear}(i,j)
\mathcal T_{cloud}(i,j).
\tag{3.4.72}
$$

## 6. Longwave Vertical Integration: Every Layer Both Absorbs and Emits

"Longwave vertical integration" propagates the thermal radiation emitted by the surface and by every
atmospheric layer along the vertical direction, and determines the upward flux $U_l$ and downward
flux $D_l$ at every layer interface.

### 6.1 Start with a Single Isothermal Layer

#### 6.1.1 Inputs and Outputs of One Layer

Place layer $i$ between its upper boundary $i$ and lower boundary $i+1$, and let $D$ and $U$ denote
positive flux magnitudes:

- $D_i$ enters the layer through the upper boundary, pointing downward;
- $U_{i+1}$ enters through the lower boundary, pointing upward;
- $D_{i+1}$ leaves through the lower boundary, pointing downward;
- $U_i$ leaves through the upper boundary, pointing upward.

To avoid confusion with the optical depth $\tau_\nu$ of the previous section, write $t_i$ for the
longwave transmittance of layer $i$. The corresponding absorptance is

$$
a_i=1-t_i.
$$

Atmospheric scattering is neglected in the longwave, so radiation entering the layer has only two
possible fates: a fraction $t_i$ passes through and a fraction $1-t_i$ is absorbed. The transmitted
parts of the external radiation are therefore

$$
U_i^{\mathrm{trans}}=t_iU_{i+1},
\qquad
D_{i+1}^{\mathrm{trans}}=t_iD_i.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
U_i=t_iU_{i+1}+(1-t_i)B_i
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
D_{i+1}=t_iD_i+(1-t_i)B_i
}
$$

#### 6.1.2 Heating and Cooling from the Layer Energy Budget

Isolate layer $i$ for a moment and ask only one question: how much radiative energy enters the layer
per unit time, and how much leaves it?

From the "absorption minus emission" viewpoint,

$$
\boxed{
Q_i^{LW}
=Q_i^{\mathrm{abs}}-Q_i^{\mathrm{emit}}
=D_i+U_{i+1}-D_{i+1}-U_i
}.
$$

Here $D_i$ and $U_{i+1}$ carry positive signs because they enter the layer; $D_{i+1}$ and $U_i$
carry negative signs because they leave it.

There is also an important consistency check. Suppose both sides of the layer are at the same
temperature, $D_i=U_{i+1}=B_i$; substituting into the transfer equations gives

$$
U_i=t_iB_i+(1-t_i)B_i=B_i,
$$

$$
D_{i+1}=t_iB_i+(1-t_i)B_i=B_i.
$$

Whatever the value of $t_i$, the incoming and outgoing fluxes are equal, so there is no net heating
or cooling.

### 6.2 Non-Isothermal Layers: Why Upward and Downward Need Different Effective Temperatures

The previous subsection assumed a single temperature for the whole layer, so the same $B_i$ could be
used in both directions. A real model layer can be thick and its temperature varies with height.
Upward photons reaching the layer top preferentially come from near the top, and downward photons
reaching the layer bottom preferentially come from near the bottom, because the farther a photon
travels within the layer, the more likely it is to be reabsorbed. Using one simple layer-mean
temperature for both directions therefore introduces a systematic error.

The scheme defines two directional effective Planck fluxes for the same layer (Rodgers, 1967):

- $B^u$ is the equivalent emission temperature of the whole layer for the upward flux at the layer
  top;
- $B^d$ is the equivalent emission temperature of the whole layer for the downward flux at the layer
  bottom.

These are not two real layer temperatures; they are directional weighted averages obtained by
compressing the continuous emission within the layer into a single number.

Let the pressure at the layer top be $p$ and at the layer bottom be $p+\Delta p$, and write

$$
B_t=B(p),
\qquad
B_b=B(p+\Delta p).
\tag{3.4.73}
$$

Measure distance downward from the layer top as $x=p'-p$. The scheme makes two approximations:

1. the Planck flux varies linearly with pressure within the layer;
2. the transmittance from the layer top to depth $x$ decays exponentially.

Hence

$$
B(x)=B_t+\frac{B_b-B_t}{\Delta p}x,
\tag{3.4.74}
$$

$$
\mathcal T(x)=e^{-cx}.
\tag{3.4.75}
$$

The whole-layer transmittance is written $t$:

$$
t=\mathcal T(\Delta p)=e^{-c\Delta p}.
\tag{3.4.76}
$$

#### Step-by-Step Derivation of the Upward Effective Planck Flux

Consider a thin slab of thickness $dx$ located at depth $x$. For its emission to contribute to the
layer-top flux, two things must happen in sequence:

1. the slab itself emits upward longwave radiation;
2. that radiation crosses the overlying atmosphere of thickness $x$ without being absorbed.

The slab's blackbody flux $B(x)$ therefore cannot be added to the layer-top flux directly; it must
be multiplied by the slab emissivity and by the transmittance of the overlying path. The two
factors are derived in turn.

**Step 1: the slab emissivity.**

Under the exponential-transmittance assumption, a slab of thickness $dx$ has transmittance

$$
t_{\mathrm{slice}}=e^{-c\,dx}.
$$

Since the slab is thin, $c\,dx\ll1$, and the first-order expansion of the exponential gives

$$
e^{-c\,dx}
=1-c\,dx+\frac{(c\,dx)^2}{2}-\cdots
\approx1-c\,dx.
$$

Longwave scattering is neglected, so the slab absorptance is $1-t_{\mathrm{slice}}$:

$$
a_{\mathrm{slice}}
=1-e^{-c\,dx}
\approx c\,dx.
$$

By Kirchhoff's law, emissivity equals absorptance in local thermodynamic equilibrium. The upward
longwave flux emitted by the slab is therefore

$$
dU_{\mathrm{emit}}
=B(x)a_{\mathrm{slice}}
\approx B(x)c\,dx.
$$

Here $B(x)$ sets how strongly a blackbody at temperature $T(x)$ emits, while $c\,dx$ says that this
very thin slab has only a small emissivity; their product is what the slab actually emits upward.

**Step 2: how much of that emission escapes to the layer top.**

The slab lies at depth $x$ below the layer top. The transmittance of the overlying path from the
slab to the layer top is

$$
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{above}}(x)=e^{-cx}.
$$

Only the fraction $e^{-cx}$ of the slab's upward emission reaches the layer top without being
reabsorbed by the overlying air. The slab's actual contribution to the layer-top upward flux is
therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
dU_{\mathrm{top}}(x)
&=\underbrace{B(x)}_{\text{local blackbody flux}}
\underbrace{c\,dx}_{\text{slab emissivity}}
\underbrace{e^{-cx}}_{\text{overlying transmittance}}\\[3pt]
&=B(x)ce^{-cx}dx.
\end{aligned}
$$

That is where $B(x)ce^{-cx}dx$ comes from. The three factors have distinct roles: $B(x)$ determines
how strongly the slab "can" emit at that temperature, $c\,dx$ determines what fraction of the
blackbody flux it actually emits, and $e^{-cx}$ determines how much of that emission finally
reaches the layer top.

Summing the contributions of all slabs from $x=0$ to $x=\Delta p$ inside the layer means integrating:

$$
\Delta U
=\int_0^{\Delta p}B(x)ce^{-cx}dx.
\tag{3.4.77}
$$

Define the upward effective Planck flux $B^u$ so that the whole layer acts as a uniform layer of
emissivity $1-t$:

$$
\Delta U=B^u(1-t).
\tag{3.4.78}
$$

Substituting Eq. (3.4.74) into Eq. (3.4.77),

$$
\Delta U
=B_t\int_0^{\Delta p}ce^{-cx}dx
+\frac{B_b-B_t}{\Delta p}
\int_0^{\Delta p}cxe^{-cx}dx.
\tag{3.4.79}
$$

Omitting the intermediate algebra, the result is

$$
B^u
=\frac{B_t-tB_b}{1-t}
+\frac{B_t-B_b}{\ln t}.
\tag{3.4.80}
$$

This result embodies "the closer to the layer top, the easier the upward radiation escapes": $B^u$
weights $B_t$ more heavily.

#### Downward Effective Planck Flux

Repeating the same derivation for the downward radiation reaching the layer bottom, with the layer
top and bottom exchanged, gives

$$
B^d=
\frac{B_b-tB_t}{1-t}
+\frac{B_b-B_t}{\ln t},
\tag{3.4.81}
$$

Adding Eqs. (3.4.80) and (3.4.81) cancels the terms containing $\ln t$ and leaves

$$
B^u+B^d=B_t+B_b.
\tag{3.4.82}
$$

The implementation computes $B^d$ first and then obtains $B^u$ from

$$
B^u=B_t+B_b-B^d.
\tag{3.4.83}
$$

#### Checking the Formulas with Limiting Cases

The expressions look complicated, but two limits are intuitive:

- as $t\rightarrow0$, the layer is almost opaque, only emission near its boundaries escapes, and
  $B^u\rightarrow B_t$, $B^d\rightarrow B_b$;
- as $t\rightarrow1$, the layer is transparent and emits little; after normalization the effective
  emission tends to the average of the top and bottom values,
  $B^u\rightarrow(B_t+B_b)/2$, $B^d\rightarrow(B_t+B_b)/2$.

When the temperature profile within the layer is not monotonic, so that the layer-center Planck flux
falls outside the range spanned by the two interface values, the linear assumption breaks down and
the scheme switches to symmetric weighting:

$$
B^u=B^d
=\frac12B_m+\frac14(B_t+B_b),
\tag{3.4.84}
$$

where $B_m$ is the Planck flux at the layer center. This fallback prevents an unreasonable
directional temperature from being extrapolated near an inversion or a temperature extremum.

### 6.3 From One Layer to the Whole Column

#### 6.3.1 A Two-Layer Recursion First

Let the top of the atmosphere, the interface between the two layers, and the surface be interfaces $0$, $1$, $2$. The upper layer has transmittance and Planck flux $t_0,B_0$, the lower layer $t_1,B_1$. For simplicity, assume that both components have uniform temperatures and that the surface behaves as a perfect blackbody. Under these assumptions, the upward longwave radiation from the surface, \(U_2\), is equal to the surface blackbody emission \(B_s\).

The downward calculation starts at the top of the atmosphere. Space supplies almost no thermal
infrared radiation to Earth, so $D_0=0$:

$$
D_1=t_0D_0+(1-t_0)B_0=(1-t_0)B_0,
$$

$$
D_2=t_1D_1+(1-t_1)B_1.
$$

Then calculate upward from the surface:

$$
U_1=t_1B_s+(1-t_1)B_1,
$$

$$
U_0=t_0U_1+(1-t_0)B_0.
$$

Expanding $U_1$ reveals the three sources of the top-of-atmosphere upward flux:

$$
U_0=
\underbrace{t_0t_1B_s}_{\text{surface emission through both layers}}
+\underbrace{t_0(1-t_1)B_1}_{\text{lower-layer emission through the upper layer}}
+\underbrace{(1-t_0)B_0}_{\text{upper-layer emission}}.
$$

This is the core of vertical integration: for a target interface, all possible emission sources add
up, and each source contributes "how much it emitted" times "how much can still be transmitted from
the source to the target".

#### 6.3.2 Why the Broadband Scheme Uses the Difference of Cumulative Transmittances

The most easily confused point here is that **single-frequency transmittances can be multiplied
along a path, but frequency-integrated broadband transmittances generally cannot.** The "difference
of cumulative transmittances" is precisely what preserves the different absorption locations of
different frequencies in a broadband calculation without constructing an unreliable single-layer
broadband transmittance.

Number the atmospheric interfaces $0,1,\ldots,L$, with 0 at the top of the atmosphere and $L$ at the
surface; layer $j+1/2$ lies between interfaces $j$ and $j+1$. Consider its contribution to the
upward flux at a target interface $l$ above it, with $l\le j$.

##### Step 1: The "Transmittance Difference" at a Single Frequency

At frequency $\nu$, let

- $T_\nu(l,j)$ be the cumulative transmittance from interface $j$ to interface $l$;
- $t_{\nu,j}$ be the transmittance of layer $j+1/2$ itself.

The path from interface $j+1$ to $l$ crosses layer $j+1/2$ in addition to the path from interface
$j$ to $l$, so

$$
T_\nu(l,j+1)=T_\nu(l,j)t_{\nu,j}.
$$

The layer emissivity is $1-t_{\nu,j}$. The radiation it emits upward must still cross the overlying
atmosphere between interfaces $j$ and $l$, so its weight at $l$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\underbrace{T_\nu(l,j)}_{\text{overlying transmittance}}
\underbrace{(1-t_{\nu,j})}_{\text{layer emissivity}}
&=T_\nu(l,j)-T_\nu(l,j)t_{\nu,j}\\
&=T_\nu(l,j)-T_\nu(l,j+1).
\end{aligned}
$$

The difference can therefore be read as comparing two paths: the first ends at interface $j$, the second extends one layer farther down. **The reduction in cumulative transmittance is exactly the absorption contribution of layer $j+1/2$ at that frequency; by Kirchhoff's law it is also the weight with which that layer emits and successfully reaches the target interface.**

##### Step 2: Summing the Contributions of All Layers

The broadband scheme uses the directional effective Planck flux $B_{j+1/2}^u$ to represent the
upward emission strength of layer $j+1/2$. The contribution of that layer to interface $l$ is
therefore

$$
\Delta U_{j+1/2\rightarrow l}
=B_{j+1/2}^u
\left[
\mathcal T(l,j)-\mathcal T(l,j+1)
\right].
\tag{3.4.85}
$$

Summing the contributions of all atmospheric layers below interface $l$ and of the surface gives
the upward flux:

$$
U_l=
\sum_{j=l}^{L-1}
B_{j+1/2}^u
\left[
\mathcal T(l,j)-\mathcal T(l,j+1)
\right]
+\mathcal T(l,L)U_s,
\tag{3.4.86}
$$

where $\mathcal T(l,L)U_s$ is the part of the surface radiation that survives the full atmosphere
between the surface and interface $l$. For every layer in the sum, $\mathcal T(l,j+1)$ corresponds
to a longer path, so the bracket is non-negative.

Similarly, summing the contributions of all atmospheric layers above interface $l$ gives the
downward flux:

$$
D_l=
\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}
B_{j+1/2}^d
\left[
\mathcal T(j+1,l)-\mathcal T(j,l)
\right].
\tag{3.4.87}
$$

In the downward case the path from interface $j+1$ to $l$ crosses one layer fewer than the path from
$j$ to $l$, so $\mathcal T(j+1,l)\ge\mathcal T(j,l)$ and the weight in Eq. (3.4.87) is again
non-negative.

In continuous form the radiative transfer integral uses the infinitesimal decrease of the path
transmittance, $-d\mathcal T$, as the local emission weight. Once the atmosphere is divided into
layers of finite thickness, $-d\mathcal T$ becomes the finite difference of adjacent cumulative
transmittances, $\mathcal T_{\mathrm{near}}-\mathcal T_{\mathrm{far}}$. The "difference of
cumulative transmittances" is therefore not a mere algebraic trick but the natural discrete
counterpart of the continuous radiative transfer integral.

!!! tip "One sentence is enough for this subsection"

    Single-layer broadband transmittances cannot be multiplied reliably, but the broadband
    transmittances of two cumulative paths can be computed separately; their difference directly
    gives the weight with which that layer's emission reaches the target interface, preserving
    absorption overlap across frequencies.

#### 6.3.3 The Top and Surface Boundaries Supply the Starting Points

Two boundary conditions are still needed to solve the whole column. The top of the atmosphere
receives no appreciable longwave downwelling radiation from space:

$$
D_{TOA}^{LW}=0.
\tag{3.4.88}
$$

The surface upward longwave flux consists of its own emission plus reflected atmospheric
downwelling radiation:

$$
U_s^{LW}
=\epsilon_s\sigma_{SB}T_s^4
+(1-\epsilon_s)D_s^{LW}.
\tag{3.4.89}
$$

The implementation uses $\epsilon_s=1$ over land and $\epsilon_s=0.98$ over every non-land column
(including ocean and sea ice). Thus about 2% of the downwelling longwave radiation is reflected back
into the atmosphere there.

### 6.4 From Interface Fluxes Back to Atmospheric Heating Rates

After all interface values $D_l$ and $U_l$ are known, form the net downward longwave flux

$$
F_l^{LW}=D_l^{LW}-U_l^{LW}.
$$

The longwave energy gained by layer $l$ is the net flux entering its top minus the net flux leaving
its bottom:

$$
Q_l^{LW}=F_l^{LW}-F_{l+1}^{LW}.
$$

Expanding $F=D-U$ gives

$$
Q_l^{LW}
=D_l+U_{l+1}-D_{l+1}-U_l,
$$

which is exactly "the sum of what enters through the two boundaries minus the sum of what leaves
through them" from the beginning of this section. 

Finally, dividing by the layer's mass per unit area and heat capacity through Eq. (3.4.4) gives the longwave radiative temperature tendency.

!!! tip "On a first reading, remember only these four steps"

    1. the top of the atmosphere supplies $D_0^{LW}=0$ and the surface supplies $U_s^{LW}$;
    2. each layer transmits part of the incident longwave radiation and absorbs the rest;
    3. each layer emits upward and downward according to its own temperature, and all contributions
       that can reach the target interface are added;
    4. comparing the net downward fluxes at adjacent interfaces determines whether the layer is
       heated or cooled.

## 7. Idealized Ozone

If no external three-dimensional ozone field is available, the scheme can use a latitude–season
total column ozone:

$$
a(t,\phi)=a_0+a_1|\sin\phi|
+a_c\sin\phi
\cos\!\left[\frac{2\pi(d-d_0)}{N_y}\right].
\tag{3.4.90}
$$

The default parameters are $a_0=0.25\ \mathrm{cm}$, $a_1=0.11\ \mathrm{cm}$,
$a_c=0.08\ \mathrm{cm}$, with seasonal phase $d_0=90$ days.

![Idealized total column ozone from Eq. (3.4.90)](<../assets/images/radiation-ozone-latitude-season.png>){: style="display: block; margin: 0.4rem auto 0.6rem;" }

Figure 3.4.4. Idealized total column ozone from Eq. (3.4.90) with the default coefficients.
**a**, Column ozone as a function of latitude and day of year. **b**, Profiles on day 90
(NH spring peak) and day 270 (SH spring peak), with their average (which removes the
seasonal term) and the shaded seasonal range. Values are in Dobson units
($1\ \mathrm{DU}=10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm}$ of ozone at standard conditions).

The vertical cumulative column uses a logistic shape:

$$
u_{O_3}(h)=
\frac{a+a\exp(-h_0/H_o)}
{1+\exp[(h-h_0)/H_o]},
\tag{3.4.91}
$$

Here $a$ is the total column amount $a(t,\phi)$ from Eq. (3.4.90) at the same latitude and
time, so $u_{O_3}(h)$ is the part of the column that still lies above height $h$: it equals
$a$ at the surface ($h=0$) and tends to zero as $h$ grows. With $h_0=20\ \mathrm{km}$ and
$H_o=5\ \mathrm{km}$, the model integrates the hydrostatic relation
through the column to assign a height to every level, then differences adjacent cumulative columns
to obtain the ozone amount of each layer. The computational order is therefore

$$
\text{total column ozone}
\rightarrow
\text{cumulative vertical shape}
\rightarrow
\text{difference of adjacent cumulative columns}
\rightarrow
\text{layer ozone mixing ratios}.
\tag{3.4.92}
$$

![Idealized vertical ozone profile from Eq. (3.4.91)](<../assets/images/radiation-ozone-vertical-profile.png>){: style="display: block; margin: 0.4rem auto 0.6rem;" }

Figure 3.4.5. Idealized vertical ozone profile from Eq. (3.4.91) with $h_0=20\ \mathrm{km}$ and
$H_o=5\ \mathrm{km}$. **a**, Ozone amount per unit height as a fraction of the total column;
the profile peaks at 20 km. **b**, Fraction of the column lying above a given altitude;
about 51% of the column lies above 20 km. The model differences this cumulative curve
between adjacent levels, so the vertical shape is consistent for any total column from
Figure 3.4.4.

This procedure preserves three first-order features:

- ozone resides mainly in the stratosphere;
- the total column increases with latitude;
- the seasonal phases of the two hemispheres are opposite.

Such a distribution is adequate for low-cost climate experiments, but it cannot represent the ozone
hole, stratospheric chemistry, or synoptic-scale ozone transport.


## 8. CO₂ Forcing

More CO$_2$ lowers the clear-sky longwave transmittance (Manabe & Wetherald, 1967; Pierrehumbert,
2010). The chain of action can be summarized as

$$
CO_2\uparrow
\ \longrightarrow\
u_c\uparrow
\ \longrightarrow\
A_c\uparrow
\ \longrightarrow\
\mathcal T_{clear}\downarrow
\ \longrightarrow\
U_{TOA}^{LW}\downarrow.
$$

Once the transmittance falls, longwave radiation from the warm surface and lower atmosphere finds it
harder to reach space directly. The energy absorbed by CO$_2$ does not disappear: by Kirchhoff's law
the atmosphere re-emits it. But tropospheric temperature usually decreases with height, so the
additional CO$_2$ makes the radiation reaching space come more from higher, colder layers. In the
language of an "effective emission height", the effective emission height rises and the effective
emission temperature falls; since $B=\sigma_{SB}T^4$, the top-of-atmosphere upward longwave flux
decreases.

The same conclusion follows directly from the single-layer transfer equation:

$$
U_i=t_iU_{i+1}+(1-t_i)B_i.
$$

When the radiative source below is warmer than the layer, $U_{i+1}>B_i$. More CO$_2$ reduces $t_i$,
replacing part of the stronger warm radiation from below with weaker emission from the colder layer,
so the upward flux at the layer top decreases. Even when the center of the CO$_2$ band is nearly
saturated, the line wings and weaker lines still strengthen as concentration grows, so further
CO$_2$ increases remain effective, with a gradually diminishing marginal effect.

At the top of the atmosphere, the initial drop in outgoing longwave radiation creates a positive
radiative imbalance; the surface and atmosphere then warm and emit more thermal radiation through
$\sigma_{SB}T^4$. In the new equilibrium, outgoing longwave radiation approaches the absorbed solar
radiation again. The long-term result of higher CO$_2$ is therefore not a permanent reduction in
outgoing longwave radiation but a warmer surface–troposphere system, required to restore planetary
energy balance at a lower infrared transmittance.

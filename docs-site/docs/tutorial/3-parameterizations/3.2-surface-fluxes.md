# 3.2 Surface Fluxes { #surface-fluxes }

Surface-flux parameterization answers a very specific question: the model cannot resolve the
surface-layer eddies of a few meters to a few tens of meters, yet it must know how much momentum
the surface removes from the lowest model level in one time step, and how much heat and water
vapor the surface delivers to the atmosphere.

## 0. Overview: How One Near-Surface Column Completes Its Flux Calculation

![Surface-flux parameterization flow chart](<../assets/images/surface-flux-algorithm-flow.svg>)

Figure 3.2.1. Algorithm flow of the surface-flux parameterization within one near-surface column.

The essential logic of Figure 3.2.1 is not any single formula but the following causal chain:

$$
\text{surface / lowest-level contrast}
\longrightarrow Ri_B
\longrightarrow (C_m,C_h)
\longrightarrow (\tau,H,E)
\longrightarrow \text{lowest-level tendencies and surface budget}.
$$

The chain is implemented by four kernels in `src/physics/fluxmod_kernels.c` (flux_surface_exchange_coefficients, flux_mkstress, flux_mkshfl, flux_mkevap).

## 1. Why a Bulk Aerodynamic Formula Is Needed

The air near the ground is full of turbulent eddies. The lowest model level represents a whole
layer of air, so the parameterization does not try to rebuild individual eddies. It uses only the
bulk quantities the model can provide: the wind, temperature, and humidity of the lowest level,
the surface thermodynamic state, and the roughness.

The eddy-covariance fluxes are

$$
\tau_x=-\rho\overline{u'w'},\qquad
\tau_y=-\rho\overline{v'w'},\qquad
H=-\rho c_p\overline{w'T'},\qquad
E=-\rho\overline{w'q'},
\tag{3.2.1}
$$

all written with the downward-positive convention. The bulk formulation closes the
unknown covariances as "exchange efficiency × mean wind speed × mean difference of the
transported quantity" (Monin & Obukhov, 1954; Garratt, 1992).

To stay consistent with the PlaSiC surface budget, we define **the direction from the atmosphere
into the surface as positive**. Then

- a positive sensible heat flux means the air heats the surface, and a negative value means the
  surface heats the air;
- a positive moisture flux means water vapor is deposited from the air onto the surface, and
  evaporation is negative;
- a positive momentum flux means the atmosphere hands the corresponding component of horizontal
  momentum to the surface.


## 2. Near-Surface Air from the Lowest Model Level

### 2.1 Why the lowest-level temperature cannot be compared with the surface temperature directly

In $\sigma=p/p_s$ coordinates the Poisson relation gives

$$
\theta=T\left(\frac{p_s}{p}\right)^\kappa
=T\sigma^{-\kappa},
\qquad
\kappa=\frac{R_d}{c_{pd}}.
\tag{3.2.2}
$$

Dry-adiabatically extrapolating the lowest-level temperature $T_1$ to the surface pressure
therefore gives

$$
T_a^{(s)}=\gamma T_1,
\qquad
\gamma=\sigma_1^{-\kappa}.
\tag{3.2.3}
$$

### 2.2 Why moisture changes the buoyancy

Moist air is lighter than dry air at the same temperature and pressure. Virtual temperature

$$
T_v=T\left[1+\left(\frac{R_v}{R_d}-1\right)q\right]
\tag{3.2.4}
$$

absorbs this density effect into an equivalent temperature (Emanuel, 1994). The near-surface virtual temperature
is then

$$
T_{v,a}^{(s)}
=\gamma T_1
\left[1+\left(\frac{R_v}{R_d}-1\right)q_1\right],
\tag{3.2.5}
$$

while the surface virtual temperature is built from $T_s$ and $q_s$. The default configuration
enables this moisture correction (internally `surface_temperature_mode = 2`). The two available
extrapolation modes are described below; dry idealized experiments can use (3.2.3) alone.

### 2.3 Near-surface temperature extrapolation mode `m_T` { #surface-temperature-mode }

`m_T` is the surface-flux parameter's near-surface temperature extrapolation mode
(`flux.surface_temperature_mode` in the C state and `NTSA` in the original PlaSim namelist). It
controls the air state extrapolated from the lowest atmospheric model level and the temperature
used in the bulk Richardson number. Both modes start with the dry-adiabatic factor
$\gamma=\sigma_1^{-\kappa}$:

$$
T_{sa}^{\mathrm{dry}}=\gamma T_1.
$$

| `m_T` | Near-surface state used by the surface-flux scheme | Moisture in the Richardson-number contrast | Recommended use |
|---:|---|---|---|
| `1` | **Dry/potential-temperature mode:** $T_{sa}=T_{sa}^{\mathrm{dry}}$. The surface comparison uses $T_s$ directly. | Omitted from both the extrapolated air state and the surface state. | Dry or idealized experiments where humidity should not modify buoyancy. |
| `2` (default) | **Virtual-potential-temperature mode:** $T_{v,sa}=T_{sa}^{\mathrm{dry}}(1+\delta q_1)$, with $\delta=R_v/R_d-1$. The surface state is $T_{v,s}=T_s(1+\delta q_s)$. | Included on both sides of the contrast, so the Richardson number uses $T_{v,sa}-T_{v,s}$. | Moist atmosphere and coupled land, ocean or sea-ice experiments. |

In mode 2, the array documented in the code as `dtsa` contains the extrapolated **virtual** air
temperature. This value is also used in the lowest-layer height and exchange-coefficient
calculation. The default is set to `2` by `initialize_flux()` in
`src/physics/physics_initialization.c`.

Only `1` and `2` are documented choices. The current C kernel tests `m_T == 1` and sends any other
integer through the virtual-temperature branch; use `1` or `2` explicitly rather than relying on
that fallback behavior.

### 2.4 Representative height of the lowest model level

The hypsometric relation is

$$
\Delta z=-\frac{R_d\overline T}{g}
\ln\!\left(\frac{p_1}{p_s}\right).
\tag{3.2.6}
$$

Approximating the layer-mean temperature by the mean of the lowest-level and the extrapolated
surface values yields

$$
z_1\approx-
\frac{R_d}{2g}\left(T_1+T_a^{(s)}\right)\ln\sigma_1.
\tag{3.2.7}
$$

The lowest model level sits
$z_1$ above the surface (the code stores it in `znl`). This height enters the roughness logarithm
as $z_1/z_0$ and the buoyancy term as $g z_1$; it sets the distance over which buoyancy acts. In
the default moisture-aware mode the code evaluates (3.2.6)–(3.2.7) with $T_{v,a}^{(s)}$ in place of
$T_a^{(s)}$, which is the density-consistent choice.

## 3. The Bulk Richardson Number

The bulk Richardson number between the lowest model level and the surface is

$$
Ri_B=
\frac{g z_1\left(T_{v,a}^{(s)}-T_{v,s}\right)}
{T_{v,a}^{(s)}U^2},
\qquad U^2=u_1^2+v_1^2.
\tag{3.2.8}
$$

Following Louis (1979) and Stull (1988), the sign of $Ri_B$ (the code variable `zri`) distinguishes three regimes:

| Situation | $Ri_B$ | Physical picture | Effect on exchange |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Surface warmer or more buoyant than the air | $<0$ | Near-surface air rises, thermal convection develops | Turbulence enhanced |
| Nearly uniform virtual potential temperature | $\approx0$ | Buoyancy neither promotes nor suppresses | Nearly neutral |
| Surface colder than the air | $>0$ | Vertical displacements are pushed back | Turbulence suppressed |

With very weak winds, (3.2.8) divides by zero and the exchange can vanish altogether. PlaSiC
therefore floors the wind speed at $U_{\min}=1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$:

$$
U^2\leftarrow\max\left(U_{\min}^2,u_1^2+v_1^2\right).
\tag{3.2.9}
$$

This is both a numerical regularization and a physical statement: a zero grid-mean wind does not
exclude unresolved gusts. (Internally the floor is applied directly to $U^2$, stored in
`zabsu2`.)

## 4. Exchange Coefficients from Roughness and Stability

### 4.1 The neutral logarithmic law

Consider first the simplest reference state: the near-surface air is neither accelerated nor
suppressed by buoyancy, i.e., **neutral stratification** with $Ri_B\approx0$. The vertical
structure of the wind is then controlled by surface friction and turbulent mixing alone.

Picture the surface layer as a momentum relay between the surface and the air above it: friction
decelerates the air closest to the ground, while eddies carry faster air downward and slower air
upward. The closer to the surface, the stronger the drag and the weaker the mean wind. The
resulting increase of wind with height is not linear but approximately logarithmic — the
**neutral logarithmic wind profile** (Monin & Obukhov, 1954):

$$
U(z)=\frac{u_*}{k}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{z}{z_0}\right),
\tag{3.2.10}
$$

where $U(z)$ is the mean wind at height $z$ and $k=0.4$ is the von Kármán constant. The friction
velocity $u_*$ is not a wind speed but the velocity scale of the surface stress,
$|\boldsymbol\tau|=\rho u_*^2$: the more momentum the surface extracts from the air, the larger
$u_*$.

Textbooks usually write $U=(u_*/k)\ln(z/z_0)$ (Businger et al., 1971; Dyer, 1974), whose zero-wind extrapolation lies at $z=z_0$.
PlaSiC uses $\ln(1+z/z_0)$, which places the effective zero-wind boundary at $z=0$ and remains
well behaved very close to the surface. For $z\gg z_0$, $\ln(1+z/z_0)\approx\ln(z/z_0)$, so the
two forms nearly coincide; $z_1/z_0$ is simply the dimensionless height of the lowest model level
relative to the roughness scale.

Instead of storing $u_*$ at every point, PlaSiC expresses the neutral momentum transfer
efficiency through the exchange coefficient $C_N$:

$$
C_N=\left[
\frac{k}{\ln(1+z_1/z_0)}
\right]^2
\tag{3.2.11}
$$

(the code variable `zkblnz2`). $C_N$ is the baseline efficiency with which the surface extracts
momentum at a given wind speed: a larger $z_0$ makes the logarithm smaller and $C_N$ larger. It
describes the neutral reference state only; the actual momentum, heat, and moisture exchange is
obtained by multiplying it with the stability corrections $f_m$ and $f_h$ below.

### 4.2 Stability corrections

Momentum and scalars do not necessarily mix with the same efficiency. The scheme writes

$$
C_m=C_N f_m(Ri_B,z_1/z_0),\qquad
C_h=C_N f_h(Ri_B,z_1/z_0),
\tag{3.2.12}
$$

with empirical constants $b=c=d=5$ (Louis, 1979; see also Roeckner et al., 1992, for the ECHAM implementation).

**(i) Unstable stratification, $Ri_B\le0$.** Define the common denominator

$$
D_u=1+3bc\,C_N
\sqrt{-Ri_B\left(1+\frac{z_1}{z_0}\right)},
\tag{3.2.13}
$$

then

$$
f_m=1-\frac{2bRi_B}{D_u},\qquad
f_h=1-\frac{3bRi_B}{D_u}.
\tag{3.2.14}
$$

Since $Ri_B<0$, both factors exceed one: instability enhances exchange, and heat and moisture are
enhanced more strongly than momentum (the coefficient $3b$ versus $2b$).

**(ii) Stable stratification, $Ri_B>0$.** With

$$
s=\sqrt{1+dRi_B},
\qquad
f_m=\frac{1}{1+2bRi_B/s},
\qquad
f_h=\frac{1}{1+3bRi_Bs}.
\tag{3.2.15}
$$

Both factors decrease as stability increases, and scalar exchange is suppressed faster than
momentum. The code stores $f_m$ and $f_h$ in `zrifm` and `zrifh`.

### 4.3 Free-convection enhancement over an unstable ocean

Over a warm sea with weak mean wind, turbulence is maintained by buoyancy rather than by shear.
To keep the heat exchange from vanishing together with $U$ as $U\to0$, ocean points
(`dls < 1`) replace the unstable heat factor with a Miller-type free-convection enhancement
(Miller et al., 1992).
Define

$$
B_f=\frac{0.0016\,(T_{v,s}-T_{v,a}^{(s)})^{1/3}}
{U C_N},
\tag{3.2.16}
$$

then

$$
f_h=\left(1+B_f^{1.25}\right)^{0.8}.
\tag{3.2.17}
$$

For a small buoyancy contrast, $f_h\to1$; for strong free convection over a warm sea, $f_h$ stays
well above one even when the mean wind is weak. Because $1.25\times0.8=1$, a large $B_f$ implies
$f_h\propto B_f$, so the heat exchange velocity $V_h=UC_Nf_h$ becomes proportional to
$(T_{v,s}-T_{v,a}^{(s)})^{1/3}$ and independent of $U$ — the free-convection limit. Momentum is
not affected by this branch: it always uses (3.2.14).

### 4.4 What enters the fluxes is the exchange velocity

Absorbing the wind speed into the dimensionless coefficients defines

$$
V_m=UC_m=UC_Nf_m,
\qquad
V_h=UC_h=UC_Nf_h.
\tag{3.2.18}
$$

Both $V_m$ and $V_h$ have units of velocity (the code calls them `dtransm` and `dtransh`). 

## 5. The Three Surface Fluxes

### 5.1 Momentum flux and wind stress

With downward positive, the two stress components are

$$
\tau_x=\rho_sV_m u_1,
\qquad
\tau_y=\rho_sV_m v_1,
\qquad
\rho_s\approx\frac{p_s}{R_dT_s},
\tag{3.2.19}
$$

where $T_s$ is the surface (skin) temperature. The stress is parallel to the near-surface wind and
transfers atmospheric momentum to the surface, so the atmosphere feels the opposite reaction.
Both components are damped by the same scalar $V_m$, so surface friction reduces the wind speed
but does not rotate the wind direction. Figure 3.2.2 places this stress on the globe for one
November snapshot of the T85/L25 integration.

![Global surface wind stress magnitude and its zonal mean](<../assets/images/surface-flux-wind-stress-t85.png>)

the mean of the 47 physics steps accumulated when the restart was written (≈ 11.75 h, early
November in the model calendar). **a**, Global distribution of the stress magnitude; the arrows
give the direction of the stress vector and the reference arrow is 0.5 N m⁻². **b**, Zonal mean
split into land and ocean, with the area-weighted global, ocean and land means labelled. The
stress follows the near-surface wind of Eq. (3.2.19): the Southern Ocean storm track is the
global maximum (zonal mean 0.32 N m⁻² at 50°S, individual grid cells above 1 N m⁻²), the North
Atlantic and North Pacific storm tracks are the next strongest, and the trade-wind belts appear
as weaker but coherent bands. The equatorial doldrums and the subtropical highs are nearly calm
(zonal-mean minimum 0.017 N m⁻² at the equator). At the same latitude in the Southern
Hemisphere the land surface carries more stress than the ocean, because a rougher surface extracts more momentum from the same wind.

### 5.2 Sensible heat flux

The moist-air specific heat is approximated as

$$
c_p(q)=c_{pd}\left(1+\alpha_q q_1\right),
\qquad
\alpha_q=\frac{c_{pv}}{c_{pd}}-1.
\tag{3.2.20}
$$

The sensible heat flux, written in the same bulk form (Louis, 1979), is

$$
H=\rho_s c_pV_h\left(\gamma T_1-T_s\right)
=\rho_s c_pV_h\left(T_a^{(s)}-T_s\right).
\tag{3.2.21}
$$

If the surface is warmer than the extrapolated air, $T_a^{(s)}-T_s<0$ and hence $H<0$: energy
flows upward into the atmosphere. If the air is warmer, the surface gains sensible heat.
Figure 3.2.3 shows both signs coexisting on the same November day: the warm ocean heats the
air, and the cold land is heated by the air.

![Global sensible heat flux and its zonal mean](<../assets/images/surface-flux-sensible-heat-t85.png>)

Figure 3.2.3. Sensible heat flux $H$ from the same restart, in the downward-positive convention
of Eq. (3.2.21): red regions receive sensible heat from the air, blue regions deliver it to the
air. **a**, Global distribution; **b**, zonal mean split into land and ocean. The map is the
bulk formula made visible: 94 % of ocean cells have $H<0$, with the western boundary currents and

have $H>0$, and the coldest land surfaces — Siberia, northern Canada, Greenland and Antarctica —
are the deepest red on the map. The area-weighted global mean is −23.2 W m⁻². In the zonal mean
the three curves nearly coincide, because most of every latitude band is ocean; the exception is
the southern subtropics near 30°S, where the dry Australian and southern African land surfaces
warm the air far more strongly (zonal-mean land value −113 W m⁻²) than the ocean at the same
latitude (−26 W m⁻²).

### 5.3 Moisture and latent heat flux

Let $C_w\in[0,1]$ be the surface evaporation efficiency (the code's `plasic_dwetfac`, the wetness
factor). Then

$$
E=\rho_sV_hC_w(q_1-q_s),
\tag{3.2.22}
$$

where $q_s$ is the saturation specific humidity determined by the surface temperature and phase.
Over the ocean $C_w$ is close to one; dry soil has a much smaller $C_w$. With downward positive,
evaporation ($q_1<q_s$) gives $E<0$ and $LE<0$: the surface loses water and energy.

A liquid-water surface or a warm land surface uses the latent heat of vaporization $L_v$, while a
frozen land surface can use the latent heat of sublimation $L_s$; the difference is the latent
heat of melting that must also be supplied when vapor leaves an ice surface. The code selects
$L_v$ when $T_s$ is above the melting point or the point is ocean, and $L_s$ otherwise.
Figure 3.2.4 maps the resulting latent heat flux.

![Global latent heat flux and its zonal mean](<../assets/images/surface-flux-latent-heat-t85.png>)

The arid subtropical land surfaces are nearly white, because the
wetness factor $C_w$ of Eq. (3.2.22) shuts the flux down when the bucket holds no water. This is
the spatial counterpart of the warning that follows: the aerodynamic capacity of the air and the
actual evaporation from the ground are not the same quantity.

!!! note "Aerodynamic capacity is not actual evaporation"

    Equation (3.2.22) gives the moisture the atmosphere is able to take up. The actual evaporation
    from land cannot exceed the water currently available in the surface bucket. Strong winds,
    dry air, and a warm surface therefore do not guarantee equally large actual evaporation.

Figure 3.2.5 expresses the same exchange as an equivalent water depth, which is the form in which
evaporation enters the surface water budget, and shows where the bucket limit bites.

aerodynamic capacity produces no evaporation.

## 6. Why the Lowest Level Is Updated Implicitly

### 6.1 Momentum: from layer mass conservation

The mass per unit area of the lowest model level is

$$
M_1=\frac{p_s\Delta\sigma_1}{g}.
\tag{3.2.23}
$$

Let one physics update span $\Delta t_*$; in the PlaSiC leapfrog framework this is normally
$\Delta t_*=2\Delta t$. For the zonal wind, surface friction is discretized as

$$
M_1\frac{u_1^+-u_1^-}{\Delta t_*}
=-\rho_sV_m u_1^+.
\tag{3.2.24}
$$

Rearranging gives

$$
u_1^+=\frac{u_1^-}{1+\lambda_m},
\qquad
\lambda_m=\frac{g\Delta t_*V_m}
{R_dT_s\Delta\sigma_1}.
\tag{3.2.25}
$$

The meridional component is identical. However large $\lambda_m$ becomes, the damping factor
stays between zero and one, so friction can never overshoot and reverse the wind.

### 6.2 Temperature and moisture

During the update, treat the surface state $T_s,q_s$ and the exchange velocity $V_h$ as known.
The linearized (semi-implicit) coupling follows the ECHAM formulation (Roeckner et al., 1992).
The heat budget of the lowest level is

$$
M_1c_p\frac{T_1^+-T_1^-}{\Delta t_*}
=-
\rho_s c_pV_h\left(\gamma T_1^+-T_s\right).
$$

With

$$
\lambda_h=\frac{g\Delta t_*V_h}
{R_dT_s\Delta\sigma_1},
\tag{3.2.26}
$$

dividing by $M_1c_p/\Delta t_*$ (which cancels $c_p$) and collecting the terms in $T_1^+$ gives

$$
T_1^+-T_1^-=\lambda_h\left(T_s-\gamma T_1^+\right),
\qquad
\left(1+\lambda_h\gamma\right)T_1^+
=T_1^-+\lambda_hT_s.
$$

Hence the implicit solution for sensible heat exchange is

$$
T_1^+=\frac{T_1^-+\lambda_hT_s}
{1+\lambda_h\gamma}
\tag{3.2.27}
$$

(the code variable `ztn`). For moisture,

$$
M_1\frac{q_1^+-q_1^-}{\Delta t_*}
=-
\rho_sV_hC_w\left(q_1^+-q_s\right).
$$

Define the moisture exchange strength

$$
\lambda_q=C_w\lambda_h.
$$

Dividing the moisture budget by $M_1/\Delta t_*$ and rearranging yields

$$
q_1^+-q_1^-
=\lambda_q(q_s-q_1^+),
\qquad
(1+\lambda_q)q_1^+
=q_1^-+\lambda_q q_s.
$$

Thus the candidate solution without physical limits is

$$
q_1^*=\frac{q_1^-+\lambda_q q_s}{1+\lambda_q}
\tag{3.2.28}
$$

(the code's `implicit_q`). The evaporation closure allows this process to increase the
lowest-level specific humidity only:

$$
q_1^+=\max(q_1^-,q_1^*).
\tag{3.2.29}
$$

If $q_s<q_1^-$, the candidate would be lower than the original value, which physically means dew
deposition; this surface-evaporation scheme does not handle that direction of the flux, so (3.2.29)
clips it.

Even when $q_s>q_1^-$, the aerodynamic candidate cannot exceed what the surface can actually
supply during the update. For a surface with finite water storage, the additional constraint is

$$
M_1(q_1^+-q_1^-)
\le \text{water mass the surface can supply during the update}.
\tag{3.2.30}
$$

In other words, (3.2.28) answers "how much moisture would the air take up if water were
unlimited?", and (3.2.29)–(3.2.30) shrink the result to what the surface can truly evaporate. If the
available water mass is $\Delta m_w$, (3.2.30) is equivalent to
$q_1^+\le q_1^-+\Delta m_w/M_1$: the less water is available, the smaller the permitted humidity
increment. The cap is applied only on land (`dls > 0`) and is built from `plasic_dwatc`, the
water content of the surface bucket, converted to a mass per unit area with the density of liquid
water.

## 7. Why Frictional Dissipation Can Warm the Air

Surface stress reduces the horizontal kinetic energy of the lowest level. Over one update, the
kinetic-energy change per unit mass is

$$
\Delta K=
\frac12\left[(u_1^+)^2+(v_1^+)^2-(u_1^-)^2-(v_1^-)^2\right]\le0.
\tag{3.2.31}
$$

When dissipation heating is enabled (`plasic_ndheat > 0`), the lost mechanical energy becomes
internal energy:

$$
\left.\frac{\partial T_1}{\partial t}\right|_{\rm fric}
=-\frac{\Delta K}{\Delta t_*c_p(q_1)}\ge0.
\tag{3.2.32}
$$

The term (the code variable `zdtdt`) is usually small, but it closes the energy budget. Turning
the heating off does not remove the friction; it only means that the removed kinetic energy never
returns to the temperature field.

## 8. What the Flux Derivatives with Respect to Surface Temperature Are For

Surface temperature is often solved from a surface energy balance, while the sensible and latent
heat fluxes themselves depend on $T_s$. Updating the two explicitly in sequence would oscillate
under strong coupling. The parameterization therefore provides local linearizations
(Roeckner et al., 1992):

$$
\frac{\partial H}{\partial T_s}
\approx-\rho_sc_pV_h,
\tag{3.2.33}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial(LE)}{\partial T_s}
\approx-L\rho_sV_hC_w\frac{\partial q_s}{\partial T_s}.
\tag{3.2.34}
$$

These derivatives (`plasic_dshdt` and `plasic_dlhdt`) can be placed in the denominator of the
land, ocean, or sea-ice surface energy equation; they estimate how much the turbulent cooling
grows when the surface warms within one iteration.

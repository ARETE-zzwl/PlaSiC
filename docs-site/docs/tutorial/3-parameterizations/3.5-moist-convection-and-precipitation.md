# 3.5 Moist Convection and Precipitation Parameterization { #moist-convection-and-precipitation }

In weather and climate models, precipitation is not as simple as "remove some water vapor once the air is moist". Within one grid cell, water vapor may experience low-level convergence, parcel lifting, condensation, latent-heat release, in-cloud mixing, rain–snow phase change and evaporation during fallout; at the same time these processes alter temperature, humidity and stability, and thereby feed back on whether convection can continue.

PlaSiC's moist physics closes this chain with several interlocking parameterizations:

- **Kuo-type cumulus convection** partitions the large-scale moisture supply into convective heating, environmental moistening and convective precipitation (Kuo, 1965, 1974);
- **Shallow-convection diffusion** represents transport by low-level shallow cumulus as vertical mixing of finite strength (Tiedtke, 1983);
- **Dry convective adjustment** removes the remaining dry-adiabatic instability (Manabe & Strickler, 1964);
- **Large-scale condensation** relaxes grid-mean supersaturation back to saturation (Emanuel, 1994);
- **Precipitation fallout** handles rain–snow phase change and re-evaporation in subsaturated layers;
- **Diagnostic clouds** derive cloud cover and cloud liquid water from convective precipitation and relative humidity for the radiation scheme (Slingo & Slingo, 1991; Kiehl et al., 1996).

This note follows the physical relations implemented in `src/physics/rainmod_kernels.c`.
The focus is the **mathematical closure and physical meaning**, not a line-by-line description of functions, arrays or statements.

## 1. Why convection and precipitation must be parameterized

### 1.1 A grid cell is not a cloud

Cumulus clouds usually have horizontal scales far smaller than a climate-model grid cell. The model knows the grid-mean temperature, specific humidity and wind, but nothing about the radius, updraft speed or water content of the individual updrafts inside the cell. Even when the mean air of a grid cell is unsaturated, a small fraction of strong updrafts within it may already have formed a cumulonimbus and be producing heavy rain.

![Convection as a subgrid process that cannot be resolved explicitly within a grid cell](<../assets/images/convection-is-subgrid-scale.png>)

Figure 3.5.2. Relation between convective clouds and the model grid scale. A parameterization must not answer "what is the detailed structure of the cloud"; it must answer "what is the net effect of these unresolved clouds on the grid-mean temperature, humidity, wind and precipitation". Source from Dmitrii V. Mironov, “Parameterization of Cumulus Convection,” COSMO-CLM-ART Training (2016), “The Need for a Parameterization” slide, [PDF](https://www.hereon.de/imperia/md/assets/clm/neu3_tl3.4.pdf).

The parameterization therefore replaces unresolved in-cloud processes with relations that can be evaluated at the grid scale:

$$
\text{resolved large-scale state and forcing}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{statistical effect of subgrid clouds}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\dot T,\ \dot q,\ \dot u,\ \dot v,\ P.
\tag{3.5.1}
$$

## 2. Notation

The moist physics is coupled to the leapfrog time integration. Define the time interval over which a physics adjustment acts as

$$
\tau=2\Delta t.
\tag{3.5.2}
$$

If temperature and specific-humidity tendencies have already accumulated before the moist physics is entered, the schemes first form the predicted state

$$
\widetilde T=T+\tau\dot T_{\rm pre},
\qquad
\widetilde q=q+\tau\dot q_{\rm pre}.
\tag{3.5.3}
$$

Saturation is then tested on $(\widetilde T,\widetilde q)$, not on $(T,q)$ at the start of the time step. This matters: warming, cooling, moistening and drying already produced by radiation, turbulence or an earlier moist process all affect whether saturation is reached at this step.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $T_e,q_e$ | temperature and specific humidity of the environment |
| $T_{cl},q_{cl}$ | temperature and specific humidity of the lifted cloud parcel |
| $e_s(T)$ | saturation vapor pressure |
| $q_s(T,p)$ | saturation specific humidity |
| $RH=q/q_s$ | relative humidity |
| $A_q$ | large-scale moisture supply rate; includes moisture convergence and, near the surface, the surface source |
| $I$ | column-integrated water vapor supplied to the candidate cloud within one physics step |
| $L_v,L_s,L_f$ | latent heats of vaporization, sublimation and fusion, with $L_f=L_s-L_v$ |
| $c_p$ | specific heat of moist air at constant pressure, $c_p=c_{pd}\left[1+\left(c_{pv}/c_{pd}-1\right)q\right]$ |
| $\kappa=R_d/c_{pd}$ | dry adiabatic exponent |
| $\sigma_{Lift},\sigma_{Base},\sigma_{Top}$ | lift level, cloud base and cloud top |
| $\beta$ | fraction of the Kuo moisture supply left in the environment for moistening |
| $P_c,P_l,P_s$ | convective precipitation, large-scale precipitation and solid precipitation rates |

## 3. Basics

### 3.1 The Tetens saturation vapor pressure

The scheme uses the Tetens form for saturation vapor pressure (Tetens, 1930), with the coefficients
of Lowe (1977):

$$
e_s(T)=a_1\exp\!\left[a_2\frac{T-T_m}{T-a_3}\right],
\tag{3.5.4}
$$

with

$$
a_1=610.78\ \mathrm{Pa},\qquad
a_2=17.2693882,\qquad
a_3=35.86\ \mathrm{K},\qquad
T_m=273.16\ \mathrm{K}.
\tag{3.5.5}
$$

Its logarithmic derivative is

$$
\frac{d\ln e_s}{dT}
=\frac{a_2(T_m-a_3)}{(T-a_3)^2}.
\tag{3.5.6}
$$

The warmer the air, the more water vapor it can hold. This strong temperature dependence is also why condensation cannot simply be handled by setting $q=q_s(\widetilde T)$: the warming caused by condensation itself raises the new saturation value.

### 3.2 From saturation mixing ratio to saturation specific humidity

Define

$$
\epsilon=\frac{R_d}{R_v}.
\tag{3.5.7}
$$

At pressure $p=p_s\sigma$, the exact saturation mixing ratio is

$$
r_s=\frac{\epsilon e_s}{p-e_s},
\tag{3.5.8}
$$

which converts to saturation specific humidity as

$$
q_s=\frac{r_s}{1+r_s}
=\frac{\epsilon e_s}{p-(1-\epsilon)e_s}.
\tag{3.5.9}
$$

In practice one first forms the auxiliary quantity $\eta_s=\epsilon e_s/p$ and then multiplies by the correction factor

$$
C_q=\frac{p}{p-(1-\epsilon)e_s}
=\frac{1}{1-(1/\epsilon-1)\eta_s}.
\tag{3.5.10}
$$

Thus $q_s=\eta_sC_q$, and

$$
\boxed{
\frac{dq_s}{dT}
=q_s C_q\frac{d\ln e_s}{dT}
=q_s C_q\frac{a_2(T_m-a_3)}{(T-a_3)^2}
}.
\tag{3.5.11}
$$

!!! note "Step-by-step derivation of the first equality in (3.5.11)"

    The first equality is the **logarithmic derivative of (3.5.9)**. The reason for taking logarithms first is that the quotient in (3.5.9) becomes a difference, so the derivative can be taken term by term, and the result naturally contains $d\ln e_s/dT$, which (3.5.6) already gives us. Start from

    $$
    q_s(T,p)=\frac{\epsilon e_s(T)}{p-(1-\epsilon)e_s(T)},
    $$

    and take the natural logarithm of both sides:

    $$
    \ln q_s=\ln(\epsilon e_s)-\ln\!\left[p-(1-\epsilon)e_s\right].
    $$

    Now differentiate with respect to $T$, holding $p$ fixed. The left-hand side is $\frac{d\ln q_s}{dT}=\frac{1}{q_s}\frac{dq_s}{dT}$. On the right-hand side only $e_s$ depends on $T$: the first term gives $d\ln e_s/dT$ directly, and for the second term the chain rule produces a factor $-(1-\epsilon)\,de_s/dT$ divided by $p-(1-\epsilon)e_s$:

    $$
    \frac{1}{q_s}\frac{dq_s}{dT}
    =\frac{1}{e_s}\frac{de_s}{dT}
    +\frac{(1-\epsilon)}{p-(1-\epsilon)e_s}\frac{de_s}{dT}
    =\frac{de_s}{dT}\left[\frac{1}{e_s}+\frac{(1-\epsilon)}{p-(1-\epsilon)e_s}\right].
    $$

    Thus:

    $$
    \frac{1}{q_s}\frac{dq_s}{dT}
    =\frac{de_s}{dT}\frac{p}{e_s\left[p-(1-\epsilon)e_s\right]}
    =\frac{p}{p-(1-\epsilon)e_s}\frac{d\ln e_s}{dT}
    =C_q\frac{d\ln e_s}{dT},
    $$


### 3.3 Why the saturation adjustment has a denominator

Consider a supersaturated trial state $(T^{Ad},q^{Ad})$. Let $\delta q>0$ be the condensed amount.
The saturation adjustment below is the standard first-order closure (Emanuel, 1994). Water vapor decreases and latent heat is released:

$$
q^*=q^{Ad}-\delta q,
\qquad
T^*=T^{Ad}+\frac{L}{c_p}\delta q.
\tag{3.5.12}
$$

The adjusted state should satisfy

$$
q^*=q_s(T^*,p).
\tag{3.5.13}
$$

Expand $q_s$ to first order about $T^{Ad}$:

$$
q_s(T^*)\approx q_s(T^{Ad})
+\frac{dq_s}{dT}\frac{L}{c_p}\delta q.
\tag{3.5.14}
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\delta q=
\frac{q^{Ad}-q_s(T^{Ad},p)}
{1+\dfrac{L}{c_p}\dfrac{dq_s}{dT}}
}.
\tag{3.5.15}
$$

The denominator expresses a negative feedback: if all of the initial supersaturation were condensed, the released latent heat would raise the temperature and hence the saturation specific humidity, so the amount that actually has to condense is less than $q^{Ad}-q_s(T^{Ad})$.

After the first adjustment to $(T^*,q^*)$, the scheme recomputes $q_s$ and $dq_s/dT$ and repeats equation (3.5.15) once more. This amounts to approximating the nonlinear saturation constraint by two linearizations: cheaper than a full iteration, yet it removes most of the residual supersaturation left by a single linearization.

### 3.4 Which latent heat is used below freezing?

The saturation adjustment of a cloud parcel uses the sublimation latent heat $L_s$ when $T<T_m$ and the vaporization latent heat $L_v$ in warm clouds. The large-scale condensation path, by contrast, performs its saturation adjustment with $L_v$; the condensate enters the liquid precipitation channel first and is converted between rain and snow during fallout according to the temperature of each layer. This is a computationally simple division of labor between phases; it is not equivalent to a microphysics scheme that predicts cloud ice, supercooled water and mixed-phase processes explicitly.

## 4. Kuo-type deep convection: cloud intensity closed by the moisture supply

The Kuo scheme (Kuo, 1965, 1974) first asks whether the environment permits a saturated cloud parcel with positive buoyancy, and then uses the amount of moisture that the large-scale flow supplies to the cloud to determine how strong the convection should be:

$$
\boxed{
\text{large-scale moisture supply}
\rightarrow
\text{cloud parcel forms and rises}
\rightarrow
\text{environment is heated and moistened}
\rightarrow
\text{convective precipitation}
}.
\tag{3.5.16}
$$

This is a **moisture-convergence closure** (Kuo, 1965, 1974). It differs from the CAPE-relaxation or mass-flux closures common today (Arakawa & Schubert, 1974; Tiedtke, 1989): the moisture supply is not merely a trigger condition, but controls the total magnitude of convective heating and precipitation.

### 4.2 Step one: find the lift level and the cloud base

The scheme searches upward from low levels for the first layer with a positive moisture supply:

$$
A_q>0.
\tag{3.5.17}
$$

If the lowest model layer receives a positive moisture supply and the surface air is dry-adiabatically buoyant relative to that layer, the parcel may start from the surface (the implementation lifts the surface temperature dry-adiabatically to the lowest model layer and takes the surface specific humidity as the initial parcel moisture). Otherwise, initiation at a mid-level moisture convergence zone is also allowed, which makes elevated convection above frontal zones possible without forcing every cloud to grow from the ground.

The first step from the lower level $l+1$ to the upper level $l$ is a dry-adiabatic lift:

$$
(T_{cl}^{Ad})_l
=(T_{cl})_{l+1}
\left(\frac{\sigma_l}{\sigma_{l+1}}\right)^\kappa,
\qquad
(q_{cl}^{Ad})_l=(q_{cl})_{l+1}.
\tag{3.5.18}
$$

If the lifted parcel satisfies

$$
q_{cl}^{Ad}>q_s(T_{cl}^{Ad},p_l),
\tag{3.5.19}
$$

it condenses according to equation (3.5.15) and releases latent heat. The level at which saturation first occurs defines the vicinity of the cloud base. If the adjusted parcel is still warmer than the environment,

$$
(T_{cl})_l>(T_e)_l,
\tag{3.5.20}
$$

it has positive buoyancy and the candidate cumulus may continue to grow upward.

### 4.3 Step two: find the cloud top along the moist adiabat

Above cloud base, the adjusted $(T_{cl},q_{cl})$ of the layer below is lifted dry-adiabatically to the next layer and then saturation-adjusted again. Because latent heat of condensation is released, the parcel is warmer than under a purely dry-adiabatic lift—this is the discrete expression of the fact that the moist adiabatic lapse rate is smaller than the dry one.

As long as the new layer satisfies both "saturation is reached after lifting" and "the adjusted parcel is still warmer than the environment", the cloud keeps growing. When either condition fails, the top of the previous layer becomes the cloud top $\sigma_{Top}$. The cloud top in this scheme is therefore not a fixed height prescribed in advance; it is determined jointly by the environmental stratification and the moist adiabatic path of the parcel.


### 4.4 Step three: count only the moisture that actually feeds this cloud

Over one physics step $\tau$, the column-integrated moisture supply received by the candidate cloud is

$$
\boxed{
I=\frac{\tau p_s}{g}
\int_{\sigma_{Top}}^{\sigma_{Lift}} A_q\,d\sigma
}.
\tag{3.5.21}
$$

Compared with integrating over the whole atmospheric column, using only the supply between the cloud top and the lift level matches the picture "the cloud draws its water where the moisture converges". If the lift level is the lowest layer, $A_q$ also includes the supply from surface evaporation. Only when $I>0$ does the candidate cloud produce deep-convective heating and precipitation.

### 4.5 Cloud–environment differences represent the adjustment convection wants to accomplish

Within the cloud layers define

$$
\Delta T_l=(T_{cl})_l-(T_e)_l,
\qquad
\Delta q_l=\max[q_s((T_e)_l,p_l)-(q_e)_l,0].
\tag{3.5.22}
$$

$\Delta T_l$ is the shape of the temperature increment the environment would receive if cloud air were mixed into it; $\Delta q_l$ measures how far the environment is from saturation. Integrating them with the layer mass gives two "adjustment capacities":

$$
E_T=\frac{p_s}{g}
\int_{\sigma_{Top}}^{\sigma_{Base}} c_p\Delta T\,d\sigma,
\tag{3.5.23}
$$

$$
E_q=\frac{p_s}{g}
\int_{\sigma_{Top}}^{\sigma_{Base}} L_v\Delta q\,d\sigma.
\tag{3.5.24}
$$

The latent-heat scale carried by the moisture supply is

$$
E_I=L_v I.
\tag{3.5.25}
$$

The closure problem can now be stated as follows: how should $E_I$ be divided between the heating represented by $E_T$ and the moistening represented by $E_q$?

### 4.6 Closure without β

In the original form, a single coefficient $a$:

$$
a=\frac{E_I}{E_T+E_q},
\tag{3.5.26}
$$

$$
\Delta T_l^{cl}=a\Delta T_l,
\qquad
\Delta q_l^{cl}=a\Delta q_l.
\tag{3.5.27}
$$


### 4.7 β closure: the drier the air, the more moisture goes into moistening

The modified Kuo scheme (Kuo, 1974) splits the supplied moisture into two parts:

$$
\underbrace{1-\beta}_{\text{condensation, heating and precipitation}},
\qquad
\underbrace{\beta}_{\text{left in the environment for moistening}}.
\tag{3.5.28}
$$

It first computes the cloud-layer mass-weighted mean relative humidity

$$
\overline{RH}
=\frac{\int_{\sigma_{Top}}^{\sigma_{Base}}
\min[1,q_e/q_s(T_e)]\,d\sigma}
{\int_{\sigma_{Top}}^{\sigma_{Base}}d\sigma}.
\tag{3.5.29}
$$

Then

$$
\beta=
\begin{cases}
1, & \overline{RH}<RH_c,\\[4pt]
\left(\dfrac{1-\overline{RH}}{1-RH_c}\right)^n,
& \overline{RH}\ge RH_c,
\end{cases}
\tag{3.5.30}
$$

where the standard exponent is $n=3$; alternatively $\beta$ can be prescribed directly. When $RH_c=0$, equation (3.5.30) reduces to

$$
\beta=(1-\overline{RH})^3.
\tag{3.5.31}
$$

Its physical meaning is intuitive:

- when the environment is dry, evaporation and mixing consume cloud water easily, so a larger fraction of the supply merely moistens the environment and $\beta$ is large;
- when the environment is close to saturation, little further moistening is possible, so more of the supply condenses and releases latent heat and $\beta$ is small;
- the same moisture convergence therefore tends to produce stronger precipitation in a moist environment and more "pre-moistening" in a dry one.

Define

$$
a_T=\frac{(1-\beta)E_I}{E_T},
\qquad
a_q=\frac{\beta E_I}{E_q}.
\tag{3.5.32}
$$

The temperature and specific-humidity tendencies in the cloud layers are

$$
\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\right)_{cl,l}
=\frac{a_T\Delta T_l}{\tau},
\tag{3.5.33}
$$

$$
\left(\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}\right)_{cl,l}
=\frac{a_q\Delta q_l}{\tau}-A_{q,l}.
\tag{3.5.34}
$$

The first term of (3.5.34) is the moisture returned to the environment by cloud dissipation and mixing; the second is the large-scale moisture supply consumed in forming the convective cloud. Only by keeping both terms together can one see how the Kuo scheme closes the grid-cell water budget.

### 4.8 Convective precipitation comes from the condensation corresponding to the heating

If convective heating comes from condensation of water vapor, the amount condensed in a layer can be inferred from latent-heat conservation. 

$$
\Pi_{c,l}
=\frac{a_T\Delta T_l\Delta\sigma_l}
{(L_v/c_{p,l})\tau}.
\tag{3.5.35}
$$

Convective rainfall is thus not specified independently: it uses the same $a_T\Delta T_l$ as convective heating, which keeps the latent heating and the condensed water mass mutually consistent. The actual surface precipitation must still pass through the fallout calculation of Section 8.


### 4.9 Optional momentum mixing

If convective momentum transport is enabled, the scheme first computes the cloud-layer mass-weighted mean wind and then relaxes each layer's wind toward that mean. In abstract form,

$$
\Delta u_l=\mu(\overline u-u_l),
\qquad
\Delta v_l=\mu(\overline v-v_l),
\tag{3.5.36}
$$

where $\mu$ is limited to $[0,1]$ by the ratio of the moisture supply to the cloud–environment adjustment energy. If dissipative heating is enabled as well, the kinetic energy lost by momentum mixing is returned to the temperature equation as heat. The standard configuration does not enable convective momentum mixing, but the physical closure retains this option.

## 5. Shallow convection: unresolved transport written as vertical diffusion

Deep convection requires a candidate cloud with sufficient vertical extent and a positive moisture supply. If convection is confined to low levels, or a candidate cloud exists but there is not enough moisture energy for deep-convective heating, the scheme can switch it to the shallow-convection branch (Tiedtke, 1983).

### 5.1 Gradient-diffusion approximation

Shallow cumulus transports warm, moist boundary-layer air upward and mixes drier air downward near cloud top. For any grid-mean variable $\phi$, the subgrid turbulent flux is $\overline{w'\phi'}$. The gradient-diffusion hypothesis gives

$$
\overline{w'\phi'}=-K\frac{\partial\overline\phi}{\partial z},
\tag{3.5.37}
$$

and hence

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}
=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left(K\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\right)
}.
\tag{3.5.38}
$$

The scheme mainly diffuses

$$
\phi\in\{q,\theta\},
\qquad
\theta=T\sigma^{-\kappa},
\tag{3.5.39}
$$

and optionally $u,v$. Potential temperature is used as the conserved variable for temperature because adiabatic vertical exchange conserves potential temperature rather than temperature.

### 5.2 Mixing occurs only inside shallow cloud

Inside shallow cloud the base diffusion coefficient is

$$
K_{sh}=10\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}},
\tag{3.5.40}
$$

and it is zero outside the cloud layers; shallow convection is restricted to below about $700\ \mathrm{hPa}$. The low-resolution five-level configuration switches shallow convection off, because too few layers cannot reliably distinguish a shallow cloud layer and its top structure.

The diffusion coefficient at the cloud-top interface is further multiplied by a relative-humidity jump factor (Tiedtke, 1983):

$$
K_{top}=K_{sh}
\left|
\frac{RH_k-0.8}{0.2}(RH_{k+1}-RH_k)
\right|.
\tag{3.5.41}
$$

This empirical term represents entrainment mixing through the humidity jump near cloud top: the sharper the moisture contrast across cloud top, the stronger the exchange there.

!!! note "What the cloud-top factor in (3.5.41) actually does"

    Equation (3.5.41) changes $K$ at **one interface only** — the boundary between the uppermost shallow-cloud layer and the layer directly above it, through which the cloud entrains environment air and detrains cloud air. Inside the cloud $K=K_{sh}=10\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$; outside it $K=0$; only at the cloud top is $K_{sh}$ multiplied by the factor in brackets.

    Throughout this section the index increases downward toward the ground, so $RH_k$ belongs to the **layer above the cloud** and $RH_{k+1}$ to the **uppermost cloud layer** (both relative humidities are $q$ divided by the saturation specific humidity evaluated at that layer's own temperature and pressure). The product then splits into two effects:

    - $RH_{k+1}-RH_k$ is the **humidity jump** across the interface. A shallow cloud top is close to saturation while the air above it is usually drier, so this difference measures how sharp the moisture contrast is. If the layer above were as moist as the cloud itself, the contrast — and hence the cloud-top exchange — would vanish.
    - $(RH_k-0.8)/0.2$ measures how far the air **above the cloud** is from 80% relative humidity, in units of 0.2: it is $0$ at $RH_k=0.8$, $-1$ at $RH_k=0.6$, and $-2$ at $RH_k=0.4$. The 0.8 threshold and the 0.2 scale are empirical constants of the scheme, not thermodynamic identities.


### 5.3 Why a tridiagonal system must be solved

Writing (3.5.38) in $\sigma$ coordinates in flux form and using the new-time fluxes gives

$$
\frac{\phi_j^{n+1}-\phi_j^n}{\tau}
=\frac{1}{\Delta\sigma_j}
\left[
K_{j+1/2}^{\sigma}
\frac{\phi_{j+1}^{n+1}-\phi_j^{n+1}}{\Delta\sigma_{j+1/2}}
-K_{j-1/2}^{\sigma}
\frac{\phi_j^{n+1}-\phi_{j-1}^{n+1}}{\Delta\sigma_{j-1/2}}
\right].
\tag{3.5.42}
$$

Defining

$$
A_{j\pm1/2}
=\frac{\tau K_{j\pm1/2}^{\sigma}}
{\Delta\sigma_j\Delta\sigma_{j\pm1/2}},
\tag{3.5.43}
$$

rearrangement gives

$$
-A_{j-1/2}\phi_{j-1}^{n+1}
+(1+A_{j-1/2}+A_{j+1/2})\phi_j^{n+1}
-A_{j+1/2}\phi_{j+1}^{n+1}
=\phi_j^n.
\tag{3.5.44}
$$

Each layer couples only to its two neighbors, so the whole column forms a tridiagonal linear system. The implementation solves it by forward elimination and back substitution (a Thomas algorithm); for temperature the same system is solved for the conservative variable $\theta=T\sigma^{-\kappa}$. Implicit diffusion tolerates large $K$ and long time steps without the strict stability limit of explicit diffusion. 


## 6. Dry convective adjustment: removing dry-adiabatic instability conservatively

Shallow convection handles moist low-level mixing; dry convective adjustment handles the remaining dry-adiabatic instability (Manabe & Strickler, 1964). Dry-adiabatic instability can be written as

$$
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial p}>0,
\tag{3.5.45}
$$

Such a stratification overturns rapidly; a coarse-resolution model need not resolve the details of the overturning and instead adjusts the unstable layers directly to a neutral state.

For an unstable block of layers from $l_1$ to $l_2$, a uniform potential temperature is defined as

$$
\boxed{
\theta_N=
\frac{\sum_{l=l_1}^{l_2}T_l\Delta\sigma_l}
{\sum_{l=l_1}^{l_2}\sigma_l^\kappa\Delta\sigma_l}
},
\tag{3.5.46}
$$

because the adjusted temperature is $T_l^*=\theta_N\sigma_l^\kappa$; equation (3.5.46) then guarantees

$$
\sum_l T_l^*\Delta\sigma_l
=\sum_l T_l\Delta\sigma_l,
\tag{3.5.47}
$$

i.e. the column-integrated sensible heat is conserved as long as $c_p$ is approximately uniform. The specific humidity is mixed completely:

$$
\boxed{
q_N=\frac{\sum_{l=l_1}^{l_2}q_l\Delta\sigma_l}
{\sum_{l=l_1}^{l_2}\Delta\sigma_l}
},
\tag{3.5.48}
$$

so the total water vapor of that block is also conserved.

A single mixing event can extend the unstable region upward or downward, so the scheme repeatedly scans and merges adjacent layers until no new unstable layer appears. Finally the adjustments are divided by $\tau$ to form temperature and specific-humidity tendencies:

$$
\dot T_l^{dry}=\frac{\theta_N\sigma_l^\kappa-T_l}{\tau},
\qquad
\dot q_l^{dry}=\frac{q_N-q_l}{\tau}.
\tag{3.5.49}
$$

!!! tip "Dry convective adjustment itself produces no precipitation"

    It only redistributes heat and moisture. If a layer becomes supersaturated after mixing, the large-scale condensation that follows removes the excess vapor and produces precipitation. This is precisely the physical meaning of "dry convective adjustment precedes large-scale condensation" in Figure 3.5.1.

## 7. Large-scale condensation: relaxing grid-mean supersaturation to saturation

Deep convection represents unresolved strong updrafts within the grid cell; large-scale condensation deals with a **grid-mean state that is itself supersaturated**:

$$
\widetilde q>q_s(\widetilde T,p).
\tag{3.5.50}
$$

For each layer the saturation adjustment of Section 3.3 is applied directly (Emanuel, 1994):

$$
\delta q_l=
\frac{\widetilde q_l-q_s(\widetilde T_l,p_l)}
{1+\dfrac{L_v}{c_{p,l}}\dfrac{dq_s}{dT}},
\tag{3.5.51}
$$

$$
q_l^*=\widetilde q_l-\delta q_l,
\qquad
T_l^*=\widetilde T_l+\frac{L_v}{c_{p,l}}\delta q_l.
\tag{3.5.52}
$$

The saturation value is recomputed and the adjustment is iterated once more. The corresponding tendencies are

$$
\dot q_l^{lsp}=\frac{q_l^*-\widetilde q_l}{\tau},
\qquad
\dot T_l^{lsp}=\frac{T_l^*-\widetilde T_l}{\tau}.
\tag{3.5.53}
$$

The precipitation mass flux produced in the layer, normalized by $\sigma$, is

$$
\Pi_{l,l}=\frac{\delta q_l\Delta\sigma_l}{\tau}.
\tag{3.5.54}
$$

The scheme assumes that condensate leaves the cloud layer immediately; there is no cloud-water or cloud-ice reservoir that can accumulate in time. The "cloud liquid water" diagnosed later serves radiation only and never feeds back on the precipitation formation in (3.5.54).

## 8. Precipitation fallout: phase change, re-evaporation and the surface flux

### 8.1 Accumulating from the top of the atmosphere to the ground

Every layer can generate convective precipitation $\Pi_{c,l}$ or large-scale precipitation $\Pi_{l,l}$. The fallout scheme scans downward from the highest layer, adding the flux arriving from above to the amount newly generated in the layer:

$$
\Pi_{x,l}^{in}
=\Pi_{x,l-1}^{out}+\Pi_{x,l}^{gen},
\qquad x\in\{c,l\}.
\tag{3.5.55}
$$

Convective and large-scale sources are always accumulated separately for separate output, and their liquid and solid parts are also kept apart in order to handle different latent heats and the final snowfall diagnosis.

### 8.2 Rain–snow phase change and its temperature feedback

The phase-change threshold is

$$
T_m=273.16\ \mathrm{K}.
\tag{3.5.56}
$$

If snow crosses a warm layer with $T>T_m$, it melts to rain and absorbs latent heat of fusion; if rain enters a cold layer with $T\le T_m$, it freezes to snow and releases latent heat. Because

$$
L_f=L_s-L_v,
\tag{3.5.57}
$$

the temperature tendency implied by a phase change (here $\Delta q_{phase}$ denotes the $\sigma$-integrated amount converted, so that division by $\Delta\sigma_l$ converts it to a specific-humidity change within the layer) is

$$
\Delta T_{phase}
=\mp\frac{L_f}{c_p}
\frac{\Delta q_{phase}}{\Delta\sigma_l},
\tag{3.5.58}
$$

where the minus sign applies to melting and the plus sign to freezing. In this way phase changes do not silently create or destroy energy.

### 8.3 Subsaturated air evaporates falling precipitation

If

$$
q<q_s(T,p),
\tag{3.5.59}
$$

falling raindrops or snow particles can evaporate or sublimate. For a given precipitation category, the in-layer evaporation flux computed by the scheme can be summarized as

$$
\boxed{
E_l=min\!\left[
\Pi_l^{in},
\frac{\gamma(q_s-q)\Delta\sigma_l}
{\tau\left(1+\dfrac{L}{c_p}\dfrac{dq_s}{dT}\right)}
\right]
}.
\tag{3.5.60}
$$

where $\gamma$ is an empirical evaporation-rate coefficient, $0.01$ by default for all configurations. Equation (3.5.60) contains three constraints:

1. the drier the air, i.e. the larger $q_s-q$, the greater the evaporation potential;
2. latent cooling lowers $q_s$, and the denominator corrects for this thermodynamic feedback;
3. the evaporated amount cannot exceed the falling precipitation actually present, $\Pi_l^{in}$.

The feedback after evaporation is

$$
\Pi_l^{out}=\Pi_l^{in}-E_l,
\qquad
\Delta q_l=\frac{E_l\tau}{\Delta\sigma_l},
\qquad
\Delta T_l=-\frac{L}{c_p}\Delta q_l.
\tag{3.5.61}
$$

Raindrops use $L_v$ and snow particles use $L_s$. Re-evaporation of precipitation therefore moistens and cools the sub-cloud air, and may favor cold pools and subsequent convection, although this scheme does not resolve the horizontal propagation of cold pools explicitly.

### 8.4 From the σ-normalized mass flux to precipitation depth

The rain and snow remaining below the lowest layer is:

$$
P_x^{surf}
=\frac{p_s}{g\rho_w}
\left(\Pi_{x,rain}^{out}+\Pi_{x,snow}^{out}\right),
\qquad x\in\{c,l\}.
\tag{3.5.62}
$$

The total snowfall is

$$
P_s^{surf}
=\frac{p_s}{g\rho_w}
\left(\Pi_{c,snow}^{out}+\Pi_{l,snow}^{out}\right).
\tag{3.5.63}
$$

Equation (3.5.62) gives the total water equivalent from convective and large-scale sources; equation (3.5.63) then identifies the part that reaches the ground as snow.

## 9. Cloud cover and cloud liquid water: diagnostics, not precipitation reservoirs

### 9.1 Convective cloud cover is determined by convective rainfall intensity

Following Slingo and Slingo (1991), converting the surface convective rain rate to $\mathrm{mm\,day^{-1}}$ gives the total convective cloud cover

$$
c_c=0.245+0.125\ln P_c,
\tag{3.5.64}
$$

and is clipped to

$$
0.05\le c_c\le0.8.
\tag{3.5.65}
$$

(When there is no convective rain, no convective cloud cover is diagnosed.) If a column has $N_c$ layers that generate convective rain, each such layer is given the same effective cloud fraction

$$
c_{c,l}=1-(1-c_c)^{1/N_c}.
\tag{3.5.66}
$$

Then, when these layers are combined under random overlap,

$$
1-\prod_{l=1}^{N_c}(1-c_{c,l})=c_c.
\tag{3.5.67}
$$

If the convective cloud top is above $\sigma=0.4$ and $c_c\ge0.3$, the top convective layer gains an anvil cirrus contribution:

$$
c_{anvil}=2(c_c-0.3),
\tag{3.5.68}
$$

and the final layer cloud cover is still truncated at 1. It represents the fact that the horizontal outflow at the top of strong deep convection can cover a larger area than the updraft core.

### 9.2 Stratiform cloud cover is determined by a critical relative humidity

The stratiform cloud cover follows the same framework (Slingo & Slingo, 1991). The critical relative humidity of each layer is

$$
RH_c(\sigma)=\max[0.85,\sigma,1-\sigma].
\tag{3.5.69}
$$

It has a minimum of 0.85 in the middle atmosphere and approaches 1 near the ground and at high levels. The part already occupied by convective cloud is first removed from the available relative humidity, and the non-convective cloud is then computed as

$$
RH'=RH(1-c_{c,l}),
\tag{3.5.70}
$$

$$
c_{s,l}=f_\omega
\left\{
\max\left[0,
\frac{RH'-RH_c}{1-RH_c}
\right]
\right\}^2.
\tag{3.5.71}
$$

The factor $f_\omega$ can limit cloud cover under low-level subsidence; in the standard setting $f_\omega=1$. The square makes cloud cover grow gently just above the threshold and rapidly as saturation is approached.

The total cloud cover of a layer is approximately the sum of the convective, anvil and stratiform parts, truncated at 1. The total column cloud cover uses random overlap:

$$
\boxed{
C_{tot}=1-\prod_l(1-c_l)
}.
\tag{3.5.72}
$$

### 9.3 Cloud liquid water diagnosed empirically from column moisture and height

Cloud liquid water is diagnosed after Kiehl et al. (1996). First the column precipitable water is computed:

$$
W=\frac{p_s}{g}\sum_l q_l\Delta\sigma_l,
\tag{3.5.73}
$$

then an empirical cloud-water scale height is defined (one value per column in the implementation):

$$
h_l=700\ln(1+W).
\tag{3.5.74}
$$

The layer-center height $z_l$ is obtained from the hypsometric relation. The liquid-water mass concentration inside cloud decays exponentially with height; converted to a mixing ratio it reads

$$
q_{clw,l}
=q_{clw}^{0}\exp(-z_l/h_l)
\frac{R_dT_l}{p_s\sigma_l},
\tag{3.5.75}
$$

with

$$
q_{clw}^{0}=0.21\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}.
\tag{3.5.76}
$$

The moister the column, the larger $h_l$ and the more slowly cloud water decays with height.

!!! warning "Diagnostic cloud water does not take part in the precipitation water budget of this page"

    Large-scale condensate is assumed to fall immediately; the cloud liquid water of (3.5.75) is an empirical diagnostic used by the radiation calculation. It must not be interpreted as a prognostic cloud-water reservoir that persists into the next time step and can be further converted into rain or snow.

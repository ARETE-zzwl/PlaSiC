# 3.1 Why Parameterization? { #why-parameterization }

Every model has a smallest scale it can carry. Below the grid scale, motions — boundary-layer
eddies, individual clouds, cloud droplets — still transport momentum, heat, and water, and their
net effect still appears in the resolved-scale variables the model predicts. A
**parameterization** bridges that gap: it estimates the grid-mean effect of unresolved processes
from the resolved-scale state alone. The rest of this chapter applies the idea to surface fluxes,
diffusion, radiation, and moist convection; this note states what the idea is mathematically.

## 1. The two tendency terms in one predictive equation

Write any prognostic equation symbolically as

$$
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t}
=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\Phi_R}{\partial t}}_{\text{resolved processes}}
+\underbrace{\frac{\partial\Phi_P}{\partial t}}_{\text{parameterized processes}},
\tag{3.1.1}
$$

where $\Phi$ is a resolved-scale variable (a wind component, temperature, specific humidity, ...).
The resolved term $\partial\Phi_R/\partial t$ is what the discretized dynamical core can evaluate
directly; the parameterized term $\partial\Phi_P/\partial t$ collects everything below the grid
scale.

## 2. A parameterization is an operator from resolved scales to grid-mean effect

A parameterization replaces the unknown term with an algorithm evaluated only on resolved-scale
variables:

$$
\left.\frac{\partial\Phi_P}{\partial t}\right|_{\text{model}}
=\mathcal{P}\!\left[\overline\Phi\right].
\tag{3.1.2}
$$

The operator $\mathcal{P}$ maps the resolved-scale state $\overline\Phi$ — the mean wind,
temperature, and humidity, the surface state, and the other fields the model carries — to the
grid-mean tendency produced by the unresolved processes.

![Resolved processes and algorithmically represented processes compose the total tendency of a dependent variable](<../assets/images/resolved-vs-parameterized-processes.png>)

Figure 3.1.1. The resolved processes contribute $\partial\Phi_R/\partial t$ directly, while the
algorithmically represented (parameterized) processes contribute $\partial\Phi_P/\partial t$,
computed from resolved-scale variables. Reproduced from Warner (2011), Fig. 4.1, p. 120.

Three consequences follow from (3.1.2) and the figure:

1. **The input is resolved-scale only.** The algorithm may encode detailed knowledge of
   turbulence or microphysics, but at run time it never sees the subgrid fields themselves.
2. **The output is a tendency, not a picture.** A parameterization answers "what is the net
   effect of the unresolved motion on the grid-box mean?", not "what does the subgrid motion
   look like?". Its result is a source term added to the resolved tendency, so the two are two
   parts of one budget rather than alternatives.
3. **The output becomes next step's input.** The updated resolved-scale variables feed back into
   the next evaluation of $\mathcal{P}$. This closes a loop, and it is why parameterizations
   interact with each other and with the dynamical core instead of acting as independent
   corrections (Warner, 2011, §4.1).

The operator $\mathcal{P}$ may be a closed-form formula, a look-up table, or an iteration too
costly to perform at every time step. What all cases share is the direction of the map:
resolved-scale state in, unresolved-effect tendency out.

# 9.3 The Complete Mathematics Behind gaussmod_kernels { #gaussmod_kernels }

`src/math_tools/gaussmod_kernels.c` contains only three functions, yet at model start-up it performs one basic and essential task:
**computing the nodes and weights of the Gaussian grid**. `gauss_inigau()` is called from
`src/runtime/runtime_initialization.c` as `gauss_inigau(PLASIC_NLAT, plasic_sid, plasic_gwd)`, writing its results into two global arrays:
`plasic_sid`, the sine of latitude on every latitude circle (the node $\mu_i=\sin\varphi_i$), and
`plasic_gwd`, the quadrature weight $w_i$ of every latitude circle. Consumers that need geographic latitude recover it with
$\varphi_i=\arcsin\mu_i$, converting to degrees when required (for example the monthly NetCDF latitude coordinate in
`src/runtime/monthly_netcdf.c` and the CO₂ band partitioning in `src/physics/plasic_physics.c`).

Section 5.1 of [*Spherical Harmonic Spectra & Transforms Method*](<../2-dynamical-core/2.3-spectral-transform-and-fft.md>) gives only the conclusion
that Gaussian latitudes are the zeros of $P_N$. This appendix fills in the **complete numerical algorithm and mathematical derivation**
behind that sentence.

!!! tip "Three things to remember first"

    1. The nodes $x_i$ are the zeros of the degree-$N$ Legendre polynomial $P_N(x)$; they are not equally spaced.
    2. `legendre_norm(n, x)` returns the normalized Legendre polynomial $Q_n(x)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2n+1}{2}}P_n(x)$, and `legendre_norm_deriv_reciprocal(n, x)` returns the reciprocal of its derivative $1/Q_n'(x)$; their product is the Newton correction step $Q_n/Q_n'$.
    3. The `abscissas` array stores $\mu_i=\sin\varphi_i$ (the latitude **sine**), not an
       angle; the true latitude must be recovered once more as $\varphi_i=\arcsin\mu_i$.

## 1. What the code has to solve

Gaussian quadrature approximates an integral on $[-1,1]$ by a finite weighted sum of function values:

$$
\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)\,dx
\approx
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\,f(x_i).
$$

Two kinds of quantities must be determined:

| Quantity | Symbol | Code |
|---|---|---|
| nodes (sample locations) | $x_i$ | `abscissas[i]` |
| weights (quadrature coefficients) | $w_i$ | `weights[i]` |
| number of nodes | $N$ | `latitudes` (i.e. `PLASIC_NLAT`) |

The central fact is that **$N$ nodes and $N$ weights give $2N$ degrees of freedom, exactly enough to make the rule
hold strictly for every polynomial of degree at most $2N-1$**; and these optimal nodes are precisely the zeros of the
degree-$N$ Legendre polynomial $P_N$ (standard theory in Abramowitz & Stegun, 1964; Golub & Welsch, 1969).

$$
\boxed{x_i \text{ are the zeros of } P_N,\qquad
\int_{-1}^{1}p(x)\,dx=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\,p(x_i)
\ \ \forall \deg p\le 2N-1}
$$

The whole algorithm therefore has only three steps:

1. Evaluate $P_N$ (in the normalized form $Q_N$ used by the code);
2. Find the zeros $x_i$ of $Q_N$ by Newton's method;
3. Compute the weights $w_i$ from the zeros.

## 2. The roles of the three functions

| Function | Mathematical role | Returns |
|---|---|---|
| `legendre_norm(n, x)` | normalized Legendre polynomial $Q_n(x)$ | $Q_n(x)$ |
| `legendre_norm_deriv_reciprocal(n, x)` | reciprocal derivative of $Q_n$ (file-local helper), used by Newton's method | $\dfrac{1}{Q_n'(x)}$ |
| `gauss_inigau(N, x, w)` | all zeros and weights, written into two arrays | `void` |

Their call structure:

```text
gauss_inigau(N, abscissas, weights)
    ├─ construct the asymptotic initial guess x(0) for each positive root
    ├─ Newton iteration (loop over latitudes/2 positive roots)
    │     ├─ legendre_norm(N, x)   -> Q_N(x)
    │     └─ legendre_norm_deriv_reciprocal(N, x)  -> 1/Q_N'(x)
    │     z3 = Q_N(x) / Q_N'(x) ;  x <- x - z3
    ├─ compute the weights w_i from the zeros
    └─ use symmetry to fill in -x_i and the matching weights
```

## 3. `legendre_norm`: evaluating the normalized Legendre polynomial

### 3.1 Normalization convention

`legendre_norm` does not compute the textbook $P_n$ but the normalization customary in spherical-harmonic:

$$
\boxed{Q_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2}}\,P_n(x).}
$$

With this factor $Q_n$ is **orthonormal** on $[-1,1]$:

$$
\int_{-1}^{1}Q_n(x)Q_m(x)\,dx=\delta_{nm}.
$$

The zeros are unaffected by the normalization: $Q_n(x)=0\iff P_n(x)=0$, so finding the roots of
$Q_N$ and of $P_N$ is completely equivalent.

### 3.2 Setting x = cos θ: Pₙ as a cosine series

The first line of the code is `angle = acos(point)`, i.e. $x=\cos\theta$ with $\theta=\arccos x\in[0,\pi]$.

The reason is that the Legendre polynomial $P_n(\cos\theta)$ then has a remarkably compact cosine expansion:

$$
P_n(\cos\theta)
=
\frac{1}{4^n}
\sum_{j=0}^{n}
\binom{2j}{j}
\binom{2n-2j}{n-j}
\cos\bigl((n-2j)\theta\bigr).
$$

This formula shows that $P_n(\cos\theta)$ contains only cosine orders of the same parity as $n$:

$$
n,\ n-2,\ n-4,\ \dots
$$

that is:

- for even $n$, only cosine orders $0,2,4,\dots,n$;
- for odd $n$, only cosine orders $1,3,5,\dots,n$.

This is a direct manifestation of the parity of the Legendre polynomials,

$$
P_n(-x)=(-1)^nP_n(x).
$$

The sum also pairs up: the indices $j$ and $n-j$ produce the cosine orders $n-2j$ and $-(n-2j)$, and since

$$
\cos(-\alpha)=\cos\alpha,
$$

these are the same cosine order written twice. Every order $k\ne0$ therefore receives two equal contributions, whereas
the constant order $k=0$ (present only for even $n$, at $j=n/2$) receives only one. This unpaired constant term is what
the half-weight correction in Section 3.3 takes care of.

### 3.3 The coefficient recurrence in the code

The code does not recompute the binomial coefficients of the cosine expansion from scratch each time. Starting from
the highest order $\cos(n\theta)$, it obtains the relative coefficients of

$$
\cos(n\theta),\quad
\cos((n-2)\theta),\quad
\cos((n-4)\theta),\quad \dots
$$

by a recurrence.

One point is easy to misread:

`factor = 1`

does not mean that the true coefficient of $\cos(n\theta)$ in $P_n(\cos\theta)$ equals $1$; it takes the highest-order
cosine term as the reference and **normalizes its relative coefficient to $1$**. For example,

$$
P_3(\cos\theta)
=
\frac{5}{8}\cos3\theta+\frac{3}{8}\cos\theta.
$$

Factoring out the leading coefficient $\frac{5}{8}$,

$$
P_3(\cos\theta)
=
\frac{5}{8}
\left(
\cos3\theta+\frac{3}{5}\cos\theta
\right).
$$

Considering only the ratios among the terms inside the parentheses, we therefore have

$$
f_3=1,\qquad f_1=\frac{3}{5}.
$$

The `factor` in the code is exactly this kind of relative coefficient, so starting from `factor = 1` is natural.

---

Where does the recurrence come from?

Start from the cosine expansion of the Legendre polynomial:

$$
P_n(\cos\theta)
=
\frac{1}{4^n}
\sum_{j=0}^{n}
\binom{2j}{j}
\binom{2n-2j}{n-j}
\cos((n-2j)\theta).
$$

Let the current cosine order be $k$. Since

$$
k=n-2j,
$$

we have

$$
j=\frac{n-k}{2}.
$$

The binomial part multiplying $\cos(k\theta)$ can then be written as

$$
B_k
=
\binom{n-k}{(n-k)/2}
\binom{n+k}{(n+k)/2}.
$$

Constants shared by all orders are ignored here, because the code only needs ratios of neighbouring coefficients.

When the cosine order drops from $k$ to $k-2$,

$$
B_{k-2}
=
\binom{n-k+2}{(n-k+2)/2}
\binom{n+k-2}{(n+k-2)/2}.
$$

The ratio of the two neighbouring coefficients is therefore

$$
\frac{B_{k-2}}{B_k}.
$$

Simplifying the binomial coefficients via their factorial definition gives

$$
\frac{B_{k-2}}{B_k}
=
\frac{(n-k+1)(n+k)}
{(n-k+2)(n+k-1)}.
$$

The relative coefficients thus satisfy the recurrence

$$
f_{k-2}
=
f_k
\frac{(n-k+1)(n+k)}
{(n-k+2)(n+k-1)}.
$$

This is the mathematical basis for the code's update of `factor` from the current order $k$ to the next order $k-2$.

---

To reduce repeated work, the code first defines

$$
c_k
=
\frac{(n-k+1)(n+k)}{2}.
$$

Noting that

$$
c_k+k-1
=
\frac{(n-k+2)(n+k-1)}{2},
$$

the recurrence above can be rewritten as

$$
f_{k-2}
=
f_k
\frac{c_k}{c_k+k-1}.
$$

For $n=4$ this gives $f_4=1,\ f_2=\tfrac47,\ f_0=\tfrac{18}{35}$ in turn:

| Order $k$ | $c_k$ | Next coefficient $f_{k-2}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $4$ | $\tfrac{1\cdot8}{2}=4$ | $f_2=1\cdot\tfrac{4}{4+3}=\tfrac47$ |
| $2$ | $\tfrac{3\cdot6}{2}=9$ | $f_0=\tfrac47\cdot\tfrac{9}{9+1}=\tfrac{18}{35}$ |

Before normalization these are exactly the ratios of $\cos4\theta,\cos2\theta,\cos0\theta$ in
the expansion of $P_4$ — except that the constant term still needs the halving described below.

!!! note "Why the even-order constant term is halved"

    When $n$ is even, the series ends with the constant term $\cos0=1$. In the expansion of
    Section 3.2 every cosine order $k\ne0$ is produced by **two** equal contributions — one from
    $j=(n-k)/2$ and one from its partner index $n-j$ — whereas the constant term
    ($j=n/2$) has no partner: there $j=n-j$. The loop does not distinguish this degeneracy and
    inserts the constant term into `sum` at full weight, so the code ends with

    ```c
    if (degree % 2 == 0) sum = sum - 0.5 * term;
    ```

    removing the extra half. For $n=4$, halving $f_0=\tfrac{18}{35}$ gives
    $\tfrac{9}{35}$, exactly the correct relative coefficient of the constant term in $P_4$.

### 3.4 The normalization factor and a numerical check

The `sum` obtained above retains only the relative ratios among the cosine terms. For example, for $n=3$ the code
does not produce

$$
P_3(\cos\theta)
=\frac58\cos3\theta+\frac38\cos\theta,
$$

but rather, with the highest-order coefficient set to $1$,

$$
\texttt{sum}
=\cos3\theta+\frac35\cos\theta.
$$

Before returning, the function must therefore multiply by a factor that depends only on $n$, restoring the orthonormal
$Q_n$ from this relative series. After the loop, the variable `angle` is reused to compute that factor:

```c
angle = sqrt(2.0);
for (index = 1; index <= degree; ++index)
    angle = angle * sqrt(1.0 - 0.25 / (double)(index * index));
return angle * sum;
```

To avoid confusing the reused "angle" with the normalization factor, denote the code's two final quantities by

$$
A_n
=\sqrt{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{4k^2}},
$$

$$
S_n(\theta)=\texttt{sum}.
$$

The function finally returns

$$
\mathtt{legendre\_norm}(n,\cos\theta)=A_nS_n(\theta).
$$

We now prove in three steps that

$$
\boxed{
A_nS_n(\theta)
=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2}}P_n(\cos\theta)
=Q_n(\cos\theta).
}
$$

#### Step 1: simplifying the code's product factor Aₙ

A chain of square roots is awkward to handle directly, so square $A_n$ first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_n^2
&=2\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{4k^2}\right)\\
&=2\prod_{k=1}^{n}
\frac{4k^2-1}{4k^2}\\
&=2\prod_{k=1}^{n}
\frac{(2k-1)(2k+1)}{(2k)^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Split the numerator of each term into two products:

$$
A_n^2
=2
\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{2k-1}{2k}\right]
\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{2k+1}{2k}\right].
$$

For the first product, double-factorial notation gives

$$
\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{2k-1}{2k}
=\frac{1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2n-1)}
       {2\cdot4\cdot6\cdots(2n)}
=\frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!}.
$$

Using

$$
(2n)!!=2^n n!,
\qquad
(2n-1)!!=\frac{(2n)!}{2^n n!},
$$

we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!}
&=\frac{(2n)!}{2^n n!}\cdot\frac{1}{2^n n!}\\
&=\frac{(2n)!}{2^{2n}(n!)^2}\\
&=\frac{1}{4^n}\frac{(2n)!}{n!n!}\\
&=\frac{1}{4^n}\binom{2n}{n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

To shorten what follows, write

$$
R_n=\frac{1}{4^n}\binom{2n}{n}.
$$

The second product is nearly the same; its odd product simply carries one more factor $2n+1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{2k+1}{2k}
&=\frac{3\cdot5\cdots(2n+1)}{2\cdot4\cdots(2n)}\\
&=(2n+1)\frac{1\cdot3\cdots(2n-1)}{2\cdot4\cdots(2n)}\\
&=(2n+1)R_n.
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting back into $A_n^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_n^2
&=2R_n\,(2n+1)R_n\\
&=2(2n+1)R_n^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every factor in the product defining $A_n$ is positive, so we take the positive square root:

$$
\boxed{
A_n
=R_n\sqrt{2(2n+1)}
=\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^n}\sqrt{2(2n+1)}.
}
$$


#### Step 2: the ratio between the cosine sum Sₙ and Pₙ

The full cosine expansion from Section 3.2 is

$$
P_n(\cos\theta)
=\frac{1}{4^n}
\sum_{j=0}^{n}
\binom{2j}{j}
\binom{2n-2j}{n-j}
\cos((n-2j)\theta).
$$

The highest frequency $\cos(n\theta)$ comes from the two endpoints of the sum:

- for $j=0$, the cosine term is $\cos(n\theta)$;
- for $j=n$, the cosine term is $\cos(-n\theta)=\cos(n\theta)$.

Both endpoints carry the binomial coefficient $\binom{2n}{n}$, so the total coefficient of the highest-order
cosine in $P_n(\cos\theta)$ is

$$
H_n
=\frac{2}{4^n}\binom{2n}{n}
=2R_n.
$$

The code deliberately sets this highest-order coefficient to `factor = 1` and keeps all other coefficients only
relative to it. The computed $S_n$ therefore satisfies

$$
P_n(\cos\theta)=H_nS_n(\theta),
$$

that is,

$$
\boxed{
S_n(\theta)
=\frac{P_n(\cos\theta)}{H_n}
=\frac{1}{2R_n}P_n(\cos\theta)
=\frac{4^n}{2\binom{2n}{n}}P_n(\cos\theta).
}
$$

When $n$ is even, the constant term at $j=n/2$ has no partner. The code's
`sum -= 0.5 * term` halves it, so that the same relation holds for even $n$ too. In particular, for $n=0$ the code
gives $S_0=1/2$, which still satisfies $S_0=P_0/(2R_0)=1/2$.

#### Step 3: the product of Aₙ and Sₙ

Multiplying the results of the two previous steps:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_nS_n(\theta)
&=\left[
\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^n}\sqrt{2(2n+1)}
\right]
\left[
\frac{4^n}{2\binom{2n}{n}}P_n(\cos\theta)
\right]\\[4pt]
&=\frac{\sqrt{2(2n+1)}}{2}P_n(\cos\theta)\\[4pt]
&=\sqrt{\frac{2(2n+1)}{4}}P_n(\cos\theta)\\[4pt]
&=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2}}P_n(\cos\theta)\\[4pt]
&=Q_n(\cos\theta).
\end{aligned}
$$

## 4. `legendre_norm_deriv_reciprocal`: obtaining the reciprocal of Qₙ'

The helper `legendre_norm_deriv_reciprocal` is named for what it actually returns: the **reciprocal**
$1/Q_n'$ (not $Q_n'$). Returning the reciprocal is arranged so that multiplying it by `legendre_norm` gives the
Newton correction **directly**. It constructs

$$
z
=xQ_n(x)-\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2n-1}}\,Q_{n-1}(x).
$$

Substituting $Q_n=\sqrt{\tfrac{2n+1}{2}}P_n$ and $Q_{n-1}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2n-1}{2}}P_{n-1}$ simplifies the cross factor:

$$
\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2n-1}}\,Q_{n-1}
=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2}}\,P_{n-1},
\qquad
z=\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2}}\big[xP_n(x)-P_{n-1}(x)\big].
$$

Substituting the standard Legendre identity (Abramowitz & Stegun, 1964, §8.5)

$$
\boxed{(x^2-1)P_n'(x)=n\big[xP_n(x)-P_{n-1}(x)\big],}
$$

we obtain

$$
z=\frac{x^2-1}{n}\sqrt{\frac{2n+1}{2}}P_n'(x)
=\frac{x^2-1}{n}\,Q_n'(x).
$$

Taking reciprocals,

$$
\boxed{\frac{1}{Q_n'(x)}=\frac{x^2-1}{nz},}
$$

which is exactly the `(point*point - 1.0) / (degree * z)` returned by `legendre_norm_deriv_reciprocal`.

## 5. Newton iteration and the initial estimate

### 5.1 The Newton update

The goal is to find a zero of $Q_N(x)=0$. Newton's method in standard form is (Press et al., 2007)

$$
x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{Q_N(x_k)}{Q_N'(x_k)}.
$$

In the code one iteration occupies two lines:

```c
z3 = legendre_norm(latitudes, z2) *
     legendre_norm_deriv_reciprocal(latitudes, z2);         // = Q_N / Q_N'
z2 = z2 - z3;                                               // = x - Q_N/Q_N'
```

`legendre_norm` supplies $Q_N$ and `legendre_norm_deriv_reciprocal` supplies $1/Q_N'$, so their product is the
correction. The iteration stops
when `fabs(z3) < 1e-16` (close to double machine precision), or after at most 50 iterations.

### 5.2 The asymptotic initial guess

Newton's method needs a good initial guess. Omitting the mathematical derivation, the angle of the $i$-th positive
root is initialized as

$$
\boxed{\theta_i\approx\frac{(4i-1)\pi}{4N+2}.}
$$

The code's `z0 = pi/(2N+1)` and `z2 = z0*(2*latitude - 0.5)` give exactly
$\tfrac{\pi}{2N+1}(2i-\tfrac12)=\tfrac{(4i-1)\pi}{4N+2}$.

On top of this, the code adds a first-order correction:

```c
z1 = 1.0 / (latitudes * latitudes * 8);        // = 1/(8N²)
z2 = cos(z2 + z1 / tan(z2));                   // cos(θ + 1/(8N² tan θ))
```

that is,

$$
x_i^{(0)}
=\cos\!\left(\theta_i+\frac{1}{8N^2\tan\theta_i}\right).
$$

For a small correction, the first-order expansion $\cos(\theta+\delta)\approx\cos\theta-\delta\sin\theta$ gives

$$
x_i^{(0)}\approx\cos\theta_i-\frac{\sin\theta_i}{8N^2\tan\theta_i}
=\cos\theta_i\left(1-\frac{1}{8N^2}\right),
$$

which is the standard leading asymptotic form of the Legendre zeros (the "$1-\tfrac{1}{8N^2}$ contraction";
see Abramowitz & Stegun, 1964, §22.16; Szegő, 1975). The correction brings the initial guess closer to the true root,
so that normally only a few Newton iterations are needed to reach machine precision.

## 6. Computing the weights

The standard Gaussian weight formula is

$$
\boxed{w_i=\frac{2}{(1-x_i^2)\big[P_N'(x_i)\big]^2}.}
$$

The code does not evaluate it directly; it first simplifies it at a zero using the identity from Section 4. Since
$P_N(x_i)=0$,

$$
(x_i^2-1)P_N'(x_i)=N\big[x_iP_N(x_i)-P_{N-1}(x_i)\big]
=-N P_{N-1}(x_i),
$$

hence

$$
(1-x_i^2)P_N'(x_i)=NP_{N-1}(x_i).
$$

Substituting this back into the weight formula to eliminate $P_N'$:

$$
\boxed{w_i=\frac{2(1-x_i^2)}{N^2\big[P_{N-1}(x_i)\big]^2}.}
$$

This is exactly the form computed by the code. Consider these lines in `gauss_inigau`:

```c
z4 = 2.0 / (latitudes * latitudes);                 // = 2/N²
z5 = legendre_norm(latitudes - 1, z2) / sqrt(latitudes - 0.5);
weights[...] = z4 * (1.0 - z2*z2) / (z5*z5);
```

`legendre_norm(N-1, z2)` inside `z5` returns $Q_{N-1}(x_i)$, divided by $\sqrt{N-\tfrac12}$.
Note that

$$
Q_{N-1}=\sqrt{\frac{2N-1}{2}}P_{N-1},
\qquad
\sqrt{N-\tfrac12}=\sqrt{\frac{2N-1}{2}},
$$

the two factors cancel exactly, so **$z_5$ is precisely $P_{N-1}(x_i)$**. 

## 7. Symmetry: why only half of the zeros are computed

$P_N(-x)=(-1)^NP_N(x)$, so the zeros are symmetric about $x=0$:

$$
x_{N+1-i}=-x_i.
$$

In the weight formula, $x_i$ enters only through $x_i^2$ and $\big[P_N'(x_i)\big]^2$, so

$$
w_{N+1-i}=w_i.
$$

The code therefore computes only the **positive** roots $i=1,\dots,N/2$; each root $+z_2$ is written into the
output together with $-z_2$ and the matching weight, halving the work.

!!! note "Why the number of latitudes must be even"

    If $N$ were odd, $P_N$ would have one more zero at $x=0$ besides the $\pm$ pairs. The loop
    `for (latitude = 1; latitude <= latitudes / 2; ++latitude)` visits only the $N/2$ positive roots,
    so for odd $N$ the central node $x=0$ and its weight would never be written into
    `abscissas` and `weights`. PlaSiC therefore always builds with an even `PLASIC_NLAT`.

## 8. From quadrature nodes to Gaussian latitudes

Meridional integrals on the sphere are usually written with $\mu=\sin\varphi$ as the variable
($\varphi$ is geographic latitude). The area element satisfies
$d\Omega=\cos\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\lambda=d\mu\,d\lambda$, so

$$
\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}f(\varphi)\cos\varphi\,d\varphi
=\int_{-1}^{1}f(\arcsin\mu)\,d\mu
\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\,f(\arcsin\mu_i).
$$

Hence the nodes $x_i$ in `abscissas` are precisely $\mu_i=\sin\varphi_i$, and the true Gaussian latitudes are

$$
\boxed{\varphi_i=\arcsin x_i,\qquad
\varphi_i^{\circ}=\arcsin x_i\cdot\frac{180}{\pi}.}
$$

This is why callers that need degrees convert `plasic_sid` with `asin(...) * 180/pi`, for instance the monthly NetCDF
latitude coordinate (`src/runtime/monthly_netcdf.c`) and the CO₂ band partitioning (`src/physics/plasic_physics.c`):
`gauss_inigau` only produces $\sin\varphi$, and converting it to angles is the caller's responsibility. The Gaussian
nodes never include the poles — all zeros of $P_N$ lie in the open interval $(-1,1)$, so
$|\varphi_i|<90^\circ$.
